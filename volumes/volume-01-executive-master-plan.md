## 1. Executive Summary

### 1.1 The concept in one paragraph

RC WORLD is a purpose-built outdoor theme park where the vehicles are radio-controlled and the customers are the operators. On 4.8 hectares (~12 acres) of leased peri-urban land, the park recreates a working industrial world at miniature scale: a motorsport complex with three engineered tracks, an open-pit mine where customers load real aggregate into articulated dump trucks, a commercial farm with tractors and implements, and — from Phase 2 — a netted airfield and a marine pond complex. Roughly 150 powered vehicles at opening, every one fitted with an in-house telemetry node, are rented in 20-minute blocks called **Shifts**, managed end-to-end by a custom enterprise platform (**RC WORLD OS**), and kept in service by an on-site engineering workshop (**The Works**) staffed by technicians the customers know as **Artisans**. It is not a hobby shop with a track behind it and it is not an arcade; it is a **miniaturized industrial complex** run as a location-based entertainment business, and to our knowledge no directly comparable venue exists at this scope anywhere in the world (Volume 2, Chapter 6).

### 1.2 The market

RC WORLD sits at the intersection of two markets and monetizes the gap between them. The first is the radio-control equipment industry: industry analysts (2025–2026) place the global RC car market between **$1.5 B and $6.2 B** depending on scope, growing at 5.8–7.5% annually, with hobby radio control overall at roughly **$3.2–7.0 B**; the toy-grade segment is shrinking in share while serviceable, tunable hobby-grade equipment grows. The second is location-based entertainment — the experience economy of karting halls, trampoline parks, family entertainment centers, and competitive-socializing venues, into which consumers direct a rising share of discretionary spend (Volume 2, Chapter 5). The gap: the RC industry sells millions of capable machines a year into a world with almost no purpose-built public venues to use them, while the experience-economy industry keeps recycling the same dozen formats. RC WORLD occupies that white space with an experience nobody else offers: families try RC without buying anything; hobbyists get engineered terrain they cannot build at home; corporate groups get a team event ("run a mine together") that is genuinely novel.

Demand is modeled bottom-up, not from market share. Volume 2, Chapter 7 builds a catchment model for a mid-size international city — resident families within a 45–60-minute drive, the local hobbyist community, schools, and the corporate-events market — and shows that the canonical Year-1 revenue of ≈$1.28 M requires a capture rate of the addressable catchment that sits comfortably below what established family-entertainment formats routinely achieve in comparable cities. The model needs no assumption that RC WORLD converts the general public into hobbyists; it needs a fraction of the people who already pay for karting, trampolines, and mini-golf to try the substantially more novel option once, and the retention machinery (Section 4.4) to do its work from there.

### 1.3 The model

The business runs on a high-utilization thesis: commercial-grade, modifiable RC equipment is now so cheap and repairable that a $75 wholesale buggy earning $15 per 20-minute Shift repays its capital cost in a weekend and everything after maintenance is margin. Nine revenue engines stack on that base — Shift rentals, memberships, corporate events, birthday parties, education programs, F&B, retail, repair services, and sponsorship — with franchising as a tenth from Phase 3. Three proprietary systems protect the margin: the **RCW Node** telemetry board (under $15 per unit, built in-house) that meters usage, enforces geofences, and kill-switches any vehicle before a customer can damage a battery; **RC WORLD OS**, the Kotlin/Supabase ERP that runs bookings, fleet, payments, payroll, and live telemetry with role-based interfaces on Android and iOS; and a gamification layer — driver licenses, badges, leaderboards, the **Gears** loyalty currency, and the **Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol** that converts breakdowns into gameplay — engineered to turn first visits into repeat visits (Chapter 4; Volume 9).

### 1.4 The ask and the plan

RC WORLD seeks **$2.6 M** — Phase 1 capital expenditure of $1,850 K (pre-opening costs and a 10% contingency capitalized inside it) plus working capital, operating funding through break-even, corporate costs, and a board-controlled reserve — released in three milestone-gated tranches: $1.2 M at close, $850 K about five months before opening, $550 K at opening (Chapter 6). Phase 1 ("Core Park", Months 0–12) opens the Motorsport and Construction Divisions, The Works, and the entry pavilion. Phase 2 ("Full Park", Months 13–30, capex ≈$1.15 M) adds the Aviation and Marine Divisions, a full restaurant, and grandstand viewing, funded substantially from operating cash flow. Phase 3 ("Destination & Beyond", Months 31–60, capex ≈$0.9 M) adds an indoor all-weather arena, the RC Academy, a corporate event centre, night racing, and the franchise pilot. Five-year total capex ≈**$3.9 M** (Volume 10).

### 1.5 The returns

Base case (Volume 10 is the source of truth): Year 1 revenue ≈**$1.28 M**, growing to ≈$2.6 M in Year 3 and ≈$3.4 M in Year 5, with steady-state EBITDA margins of **24–28% from Year 3**. Monthly operating break-even is reached in **operating Month 16–19** (operating Month 1 = the opening month); full payback of invested capital arrives in **Year 4–5**. The modeled base-case investor outcome is ≈**1.6–2.0× MOIC** over a five-year hold — a high-single to low-double-digit IRR — with the ambitious case at ≈2.2–2.9× and the franchise and second-site options as further upside carried at zero (Chapter 6.4). Exit routes include trade sale to a location-based-entertainment operator, sale of the franchisor entity, secondary sale to growth capital, and structured founder buyback.

> **Investor Note.** The plan's conservatism is structural, not rhetorical. Revenue is modeled from bottom-up Shift capacity and catchment capture, not top-down market share; the fleet doctrine deliberately excludes fragile premium hardware from rental duty; the ask covers Phase 1 fully so that Phases 2–3 are options exercised with evidence, not obligations. The single largest risk is the Year-1 demand ramp, and Chapter 9 addresses it first.

### 1.7 Why now, why this team

Three curves crossed in the mid-2020s and made this business possible for the first time. Hobby-grade RC entry pricing collapsed (a repairable brushless 1/14 car now wholesales for $60–90); commodity IoT hardware (ESP32-class microcontrollers, sub-$5 GPS modules) made per-vehicle telemetry cost under $15 instead of hundreds; and the experience economy normalized paying $15–60 for a premium hour of operated equipment. The founder's preparation — three technical planning documents distilled in this plan's source material — covers fleet engineering, battery logistics, telemetry hardware design, and ERP architecture at a depth unusual for a pre-revenue venue business, and the operating doctrine throughout this plan (1:3 excavator-to-truck ratio, 15° haul-road limit, 3:1 battery rotation, electromechanical rental fleet) comes from that engineering-first analysis rather than from post-hoc rationalization.

## 2. Vision

### 2.1 The miniaturized industrial complex

Every RC venue that has ever failed made the same category error: it treated RC as a *toy* and built a sandbox. A sandbox — a pile of dirt, a rented excavator, no goal — exhausts its novelty in a single visit, and single-visit businesses die of marketing cost. RC WORLD's founding thesis inverts this. The park is a **miniaturized industrial complex**: every zone is a *working system* with inputs, outputs, jobs, and measurable production. The Mining Zone is not a sandpit; it is a simulated open-pit operation with graded haul roads (maximum 15° incline, an engineering limit that prevents motor burnout), a central processing hopper that weighs every load, and a live tonnage leaderboard. The Agriculture Zone is a grid-based commercial farm — pre-tilled fields, crop rows, irrigation trenches, a barn — where tractors plow, disc, and haul with tandem trailers. The tracks are engineered racing circuits with professional timing, standardized fleets, and championship structures. From Phase 2 the airfield and pond extend the same logic to air and water.

The commercial consequence of the thesis is retention. A working system generates *goals*, and goals generate return visits. The customer who moved 38 kg of aggregate in their first Shift knows exactly what they want to do next time: move 50. The driver who qualified 9th wants 5th. The License holder one badge short of an excavator endorsement books a return visit to earn it. RC WORLD is designed so that every departure gate conversation is about *next time* — and the entire gamification architecture (Volume 9) exists to make that mechanical rather than accidental.

### 2.2 Why RC WORLD transcends the hobby-sandbox model

Four structural differences separate this park from every hobby-adjacent venue that preceded it:

1. **Systems, not stations.** Zones are interdependent production circuits, not isolated activity stations. Excavators need trucks; trucks need haul roads; the hopper needs both. This creates cooperative multiplayer gameplay no single-machine rental can offer — and it is why the canonical fleet arithmetic (1:3 excavator-to-truck ratio) is an experience-design decision as much as a logistics one.
2. **Instrumentation, not supervision.** Every vehicle carries an RCW Node. Telemetry meters the Shift, scores the driving, enforces the geofence, protects the battery, and feeds the leaderboard. The park knows where every asset is, what state its battery is in, and who is driving it, in real time. Competitors would need to build this; we manufacture it for under $15 per vehicle.
3. **Progression, not admission.** The RC WORLD License program means customers hold *status* with the park — levels, endorsements, badges, Gears balances — that appreciates with every visit and is worthless anywhere else. The venue business becomes a membership business (Volume 9).
4. **An engineering company underneath.** The Works is not a repair corner; it is a production workshop with maintenance matrices, FMEA-driven spares inventory, and rebuild procedures (Volume 7) that keep fleet availability at the ≥92% operating target (Volume 10 KPI dashboard) at hobby-grade capital cost. The moat is not any single machine — it is the operating system, in both senses, that keeps 150 machines earning.

The contrast, side by side:

| Dimension | Hobby-sandbox model | RC WORLD model |
|---|---|---|
| Experience unit | Free play with a machine | A job inside a working system, scored |
| Repeat-visit driver | Novelty (decaying) | Goals, status, progression (compounding) |
| Fleet knowledge | Staff memory | Per-asset telemetry, runtime hours, maintenance logs |
| Battery management | Charged when someone remembers | 3:1 rotation, bunkered, telemetry-enforced window |
| Breakdown | Staff walks out, apologizes | Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol — gameplay |
| Revenue model | Ticket per session | Nine stacked engines on a recurring-membership base |
| Replicability | Depends on the founder being present | Documented to franchise depth (this plan) |

### 2.3 The experience promise

To the customer, the promise is simple and specific: **you will operate real machines doing real work, and you will get measurably better at it.** A first-time visitor completes the Toolbox Talk induction on their phone, scans a QR code on a transmitter, and within minutes is loading a dump truck or lapping Track B. When their machine dies mid-field, they are not embarrassed by a staff walk-of-shame retrieval; they are handed the controls of a winch-equipped recovery crawler and sent to rescue it themselves — the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, friction converted into the most-photographed moment in the park. When their Shift ends, the app shows them their tonnage, their lap consistency, their smoothness score, and precisely which License badge they are closest to. The promise to parents is that this is screen-time inverted: thumbs on real controls, consequences in real physics. The promise to hobbyists is terrain, timing infrastructure, and fleet quality they cannot assemble privately. The promise to companies is the most novel two-hour team event in the city.

The promise has a fairness clause that matters more than any single feature: **the machines are equal; the drivers are not.** Racing fleets are tuned to lap-time parity within 2–3%, so a customer never loses to a better car — only to a better driver, which is a loss that motivates a return rather than a refund request. The same principle runs through the whole park: the leaderboards measure skill the telemetry can verify, the License certifies what the holder can actually do, and nothing in the progression system can be bought instead of earned. Venues that let spending substitute for skill train their best customers to leave; RC WORLD's economics depend on training them to come back.

> **Investor Note.** "Experience promise" is not brochure language; it is the retention mechanism the revenue model depends on. Volume 10's base case assumes repeat visitation consistent with mid-tier family-entertainment benchmarks — not the higher rates the gamification layer is designed to achieve. Everything Volume 9 delivers above benchmark is upside.

### 2.4 A Saturday at RC WORLD

The clearest way to test the vision is to walk it. It is 09:40 on a Saturday in Year 2. At the entry pavilion a family of four checks in; both children completed the Toolbox Talk in the car, and the app has already suggested a Family Bundle — four drivers, two Shifts each. The eight-year-old is on a Mining Zone excavator by 10:05, guided by an Artisan through her first three-channel load cycle; her father runs a dump truck in the same circuit, hauling her spoil to the hopper, because the 1:3 fleet ratio means there is always a truck seat open next to an active excavator. Across the park, a six-driver drift heat is forming on Track A — five regulars and a first-timer, all on identical MST-based chassis within 2–3% lap parity, so the regulars' advantage is skill, visible and earnable. On Track C a crawler stalls in the water hazard; its driver is already at the recovery station drawing the winch crawler, and a small crowd films the rescue. In The Works' viewing window two Artisans have a buggy on the bench, swapping a spur gear between Shifts; a customer watches, then buys the same gear set at retail for the car he owns at home. At 11:55 the app pings a Track B driver: no queue for his class — Top-Up for $9? He taps yes without lowering the transmitter. By noon, the F&B kiosk queue is the longest line in the park, which is the plan working: dwell time is the second product. Nothing in this scene requires a staff member to make a decision that RC WORLD OS has not already framed — and every element of it (the ratio, the parity, the protocol, the top-up, the sightline from the bench to the till) appears somewhere in this plan as an engineered choice.

### 2.5 The long view

The five-year vision is a **category**, not a site. Phase 1 proves the unit economics of one park; Phase 3's franchise pilot and the multi-site architecture already built into RC WORLD OS (Volume 13) convert one park into a replicable format. The end-state, described in Chapter 10, is RC WORLD as the definitive global brand for operated-miniature experiences — parks, an academy curriculum, a license program that transfers between sites, and telemetry hardware that would cost any imitator years to replicate.

## 3. Mission & Values

### 3.1 Mission statement

**To build the world's first true RC theme park — a working miniature industrial world where anyone can operate, compete, learn, and progress — run to engineering standards, priced for families, and designed to be replicated.**

The mission is bounded as deliberately as it is stated. RC WORLD is not a hobby shop with attractions attached (retail exists to serve the experience, never the reverse); it is not a technology company that happens to own a park (the RCW Node and RC WORLD OS exist to run Shifts, and their platform value is an option, not the plan); and it is not a theme park in the costumed-entertainment sense (the immersion is industrial authenticity, and the moment the park fakes what the machines do, the customers who matter most — the ones who know real machines — will know).

### 3.2 Values

The five values below are operating constraints, not wall posters. Each carries a "this means / this forbids" test that appears again in staff manuals (Volumes 7 and 9) and the franchise standards (Volume 12).

**Safety first, without theatre.** The park's safety architecture is engineered, not performed: LiPo charging happens only in the fire-isolated charging bunker within the 3.4–4.2 V/cell window; customers never set foot on a live track or field — the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol exists precisely so that the safety rule is *more* fun than the violation; telemetry kill-switches fire automatically on under-voltage or geofence breach; every zone has marshaling sightlines designed in (Volume 11). *This forbids:* any process that relies on customers reading signs, and any charging outside the bunker, ever.

**Engineering excellence.** Every fleet decision is made on serviceability and duty-cycle math, not on spec-sheet glamour. The rental construction fleet is electromechanical (lead-screw) because leak-prone hydraulics belong in the premium supervised tier; racing chassis are standardized with lap-time parity within 2–3% because fair racing is an engineering outcome; batteries rotate 3:1 because that is what the duty cycle requires. *This forbids:* buying any asset without a spares plan, and "personality" achieved through unequal hardware rather than tuning.

**Immersion.** The industrial fiction is maintained everywhere the customer looks: Shifts, not rentals; Artisans, not staff; pit stops, not battery swaps; a Toolbox Talk, not a waiver form. Immersion is the cheapest premium the park sells — it costs vocabulary and costume and returns pricing power. *This forbids:* breaking the fiction to save a sentence of explanation.

**Accessibility.** A seven-year-old, a wheelchair user, and a forty-year veteran hobbyist must all have a genuine version of the experience. Entry pricing ($15 Casual Shift) sits at cinema-ticket level; control stations are designed for seated and standing operators; machine classes are tiered so novices succeed in minutes while experts chase ceilings for years. *This forbids:* pricing or designing the park as a hobbyist clubhouse.

**Skill progression.** The park teaches, measures, and certifies. Every customer has a License trajectory; every Shift produces skill telemetry; the RC Academy (Phase 3) formalizes the curriculum. Progression is the bridge from entertainment revenue to education revenue and from customer to community. *This forbids:* any experience design in which a tenth visit feels the same as the first.

### 3.3 Organization and people

The values only exist if the organization is shaped to enforce them. The Phase 1 structure is deliberately flat: a **General Manager** accountable for the P&L; a **Works Lead** (chief Artisan) accountable for fleet availability and the maintenance program; two **Division Leads** (Motorsport; Construction) accountable for their zones' revenue, safety, and customer experience; a part-time **Finance/Administration** function that leans heavily on RC WORLD OS automation; and the front line — Artisans, marshals, and front-of-house, most cross-trained across at least two roles. Phase 2 adds Aviation and Marine leads plus an F&B manager; Phase 3 adds an Academy lead and a franchise-support function. The founder holds strategy, brand, product (RC WORLD OS roadmap), and this document.

Three people-principles carry more weight than the org chart. First, **hire wrenchers who like people, not greeters who tolerate machines** — the Artisan role is the park's brand in human form, and the hiring bar (Volume 7, Chapter 1) tests mechanical aptitude and hospitality together. Second, **the induction is the plan**: every hire's training curriculum is drawn from the volumes named in their role's reading path (Volume 0, Chapter 3), which keeps the documentation honest — if a volume cannot train a new hire, it gets revised. Third, **progression mirrors the customer's**: staff hold internal License tiers and equipment endorsements in the same system customers use, which builds credibility on the floor and gives the franchise system (Volume 12) a portable, verifiable staff-certification standard from day one.

### 3.4 Operating doctrine

Beneath the values sits a short list of non-negotiable doctrine, inherited from the founding engineering analysis and binding on every volume: the 1:3 excavator-to-dump-truck ratio; the 15° haul-road maximum incline; the 3:1 battery-to-vehicle ratio inside the 3.4–4.2 V/cell window with bunkered charging; the 20-minute Shift decoupled from battery life with ~30% buffer returned; telemetry kill-switch on under-voltage or geofence breach; no customer on a live field, ever; electromechanical rental fleet with hydraulics reserved for premium supervised experiences; standardized racing chassis with parity within 2–3%. Any proposal that contradicts doctrine must amend the style guide first — which is to say, must convince the founder and be swept through every volume.

## 4. Business Model

### 4.1 The high-utilization thesis

The business model rests on one arithmetic fact: **hobby-grade RC equipment has become so cheap, durable, and repairable that its rental payback period is measured in days, not years.** A WLtoys-class racing buggy wholesales at $60–90. It earns $15 per 20-minute Casual Shift. Eight paid Shifts — one busy Saturday afternoon — repay the vehicle. A Huina-class excavator at $350–420 wholesale repays in under thirty premium-class Shifts. Compare any other equipment-rental business: karts repay in months, boats in seasons, aircraft in years. The corollary is that **utilization, not asset cost, is the entire game**, and every proprietary system in the park exists to raise utilization or lower the cost of sustaining it:

- **RC WORLD OS** raises utilization by eliminating queue dead-time (live queue roster, T-5-minute Top-Up offers when no one waits), by dynamic scheduling of fleet across zones, and by making booking frictionless on Android and iOS.
- **The RCW Node** lowers sustaining cost by preventing the two great killers of RC fleets — over-discharged LiPo cells and lost/abused vehicles — via automated under-voltage response (push alert, throttle limited to 20%, order back to pit, kill-switch at the 3.4 V/cell floor) and geofencing.
- **The Works** lowers sustaining cost by converting maintenance from emergency to schedule: predictive flags from logged runtime hours, a spares inventory shaped by failure data, and Artisans trained to rebuild rather than replace (Volume 7).
- **The gamification layer** raises utilization on the demand side by manufacturing return visits (Section 4.4).

The F&B and retail lines are deliberately modeled at modest capture rates — they are dwell monetization, and dwell is manufactured by everything else on the list.

### 4.3 Unit economics of one vehicle-hour

The atomic unit of the business is one vehicle operating for one hour — three 20-minute Shifts. The table below shows the standard-class racing case at Year-1 anchor prices; Volume 10 carries the full class-by-class model and the canonical figures.

| Line | Basis | Per vehicle-hour |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue potential | 3 Casual Shifts × $15 | $45.00 |
| Battery amortization | ~$18–25 LiPo pack, ~200–300 cycles, 3 packs/hr | ≈ $0.25 |
| Maintenance & parts | Fleet-average consumption of tires, gears, arms, servos (Volume 7 failure data) | ≈ $1.80–3.00 |
| Vehicle depreciation | $60–90 wholesale plus hardening, over a 1,200-Shift service life with two rebuilds (Volume 3) | ≈ $0.35–0.50 |
| Charging energy | 3 × ~25 Wh at commercial tariff | < $0.05 |
| Direct labor share | Artisan + marshal time allocated across concurrent vehicles | ≈ $3.50–5.00 |
| **Direct margin** | at full utilization | **≈ $36–39 (80–87%)** |

The construction classes run the same arithmetic with different shapes. A standard rental excavator ($350–420 wholesale, $22 premium Casual Shift) earns $66 per vehicle-hour potential against even lower drivetrain wear — lead-screw machines working soil at walking pace shed almost no consumables — but adds a share of the Mining Zone's supervision labor. The single hydraulic showcase machine sits outside rental arithmetic entirely: it earns through supervised premium experiences and display value, and its economics are evaluated as marketing spend with revenue offset, not as fleet (Volume 4, Chapter 3).

Two honest caveats govern the reading of this table. First, *no fleet runs at full utilization*: park-wide effective utilization of theoretical Shift capacity is modeled in single digits for Year 1 (weekend peaks of 40–60% on popular classes, long weekday troughs), which is why fixed costs — rent, salaries, insurance, marketing — dominate the P&L and why break-even arrives in operating Month 16–19 rather than Month 3. Second, premium classes invert some lines: a $22 premium Shift on a hydraulic showcase machine carries higher supervision labor and maintenance, which is exactly why doctrine confines hydraulics to supervised premium duty. The strategic point survives both caveats: **every incremental Shift above the fixed-cost floor is ~80% margin**, so the entire management system — pricing, gamification, events, memberships, marketing — is aimed at filling troughs.

> **Investor Note.** The model's leverage is visible in one sentence: the Year-1 revenue line averages ≈$3,500 per calendar day — the equivalent of roughly 230 Casual Shifts at the standard anchor price, before any contribution from events, memberships, F&B, or retail is counted — against a theoretical Phase 1 Shift capacity more than an order of magnitude higher. The base case does not require the park to be busy; it requires it to be *found*. Sensitivities on capture rate and per-capita spend are in Volume 10, Chapter 9.

### 4.4 Gamified mechanics as retention drivers

Location-based entertainment lives or dies on repeat visitation, and RC WORLD's retention machinery is built into the product rather than bolted on as a coupon program:

- **The RC WORLD License** (Volume 9) — a tiered driver-license progression with levels earned through performance and repeat visits, and **equipment endorsements** (excavator, drift, FPV, crawler…) that unlock machine classes. Status loss-aversion is the strongest retention force in gamified systems; the License makes every lapsed month feel like surrendered progress.
- **Badges and skill telemetry.** The RCW Node scores what customers actually do — smoothness, lap consistency, clean loads, winch recoveries — so badges certify real skill, not attendance. Real certification is shareable, and shareable status is free marketing.
- **Gears**, the loyalty currency, earned on spend, performance, and milestones, redeemable against Shifts, upgrades, and retail — designed with expiry pressure and member multipliers.
- **Leaderboards and seasons.** Every zone has a live leaderboard (tonnage, lap time, harvest score); seasons reset competition and give lapsed customers a fresh start, which re-acquisition marketing exploits.
- **The Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol** — the signature conversion of friction into gameplay. A breakdown triggers an 85 dB locator buzzer; the customer hands in their transmitter and pilots a 1/10 winch-equipped recovery crawler to rescue their own vehicle, then receives a fresh machine. The park's worst moment becomes its most-shared one.
- **F1-style power management.** Telemetry reads voltage sag in real time; aggressive throttle drains the allocation faster and smooth drivers are rewarded — a skill layer that deepens with every visit and directly protects battery life. The mechanics that retain customers are the same mechanics that protect assets.

Because every mechanic runs through RC WORLD OS, retention is *measured*, not asserted: the system attributes each return visit to the mechanics that plausibly drove it (an expiring Gears balance, a near-complete badge, a season reset, a league fixture) and reports the 90-day second-visit rate and License progression velocity on the management dashboard (Section 8.5). The gamification layer is a tunable system with a feedback loop — under-performing mechanics get redesigned mid-season, not post-mortemed annually. Volume 9 specifies the rules; Volume 13 the instrumentation.

### 4.5 Pricing architecture

The price ladder is designed around three principles. First, **the entry price is an impulse price**: $15 for a Casual Shift sits at cinema-ticket level, below the psychological threshold at which a family debates the purchase, and low enough that trying RC WORLD requires no commitment to the hobby. Second, **every rung up the ladder buys more immersion, not just more time**: the Operator Shift ($26/$38) adds the pit-stop battery swap — theatre that costs the park ninety seconds of Artisan time and is remembered as the highlight; premium classes ($22 Casual) buy hydraulics, FPV, or big crawlers under closer supervision; the Day Pass ($59) and Family Bundle ($89) convert single-activity visitors into full-park explorers, which is where cross-division discovery (and second-visit intent) is manufactured. Third, **the ladder tops out in recurring revenue**: memberships (Apprentice $29, Operator $59, Foreman $99 per month) bundle included Shifts, booking priority, Gears multipliers, and License acceleration, priced so that the break-even visit frequency is two to three visits a month — below what an engaged License-chaser actually does. Local price adaptation is a Volume 10 sensitivity exercise and a Volume 12 franchise-localization procedure; the *ratios* between rungs, not the absolute dollars, are the protected design.

> **Investor Note.** The pricing anchors are Year-1 figures chosen for penetration, not for margin maximization. Volume 10 models a Year-2 price review with modest uplift on premium classes and events once the demand curve is observed. No revenue upside from that review is in the base case.

### 4.6 Demand shaping and seasonality

An outdoor venue's enemy is the empty Tuesday, and the model addresses troughs structurally rather than hoping marketing fills them. Weekday daytime belongs to **education** (school programs, from Phase 1 informally and Phase 3 via the Academy) and **corporate events** (from $1,400 for two hours, booked precisely because the park is exclusive-feeling when quiet). Weekday evenings grow into **league nights** and member sessions (night racing from Phase 3 extends this year-round). Weekends run the full retail engine, with the live queue roster and Top-Up mechanics harvesting every idle vehicle-minute. Weather resilience improves by phase — the Construction Division is the most rain-tolerant anchor (earthmoving in light rain is *more* atmospheric, and the machines are rated for it within limits set in Volume 4), Phase 2's restaurant monetizes shelter, and Phase 3's arena removes the ceiling entirely. RC WORLD OS automates weather-triggered rebooking so a rained-out Shift becomes a banked credit plus a bounce-back offer, not a refund.

### 4.7 Cost structure in brief

The Year-1 cost structure (detail: Volume 10, Chapter 5) is dominated by people (≈35–40% of revenue at maturity: roughly 18–19 FTE — 18.5 in the Volume 10 payroll model — plus casuals: General Manager, division leads, Artisans, marshals, front-of-house), then lease and utilities, then marketing (front-loaded in Years 1–2), then fleet maintenance and parts (remarkably small — single-digit percent of revenue — because the fleet doctrine was designed backwards from this line), then insurance, payments, and platform costs. The capex the customer never sees — civil works, netting, ponds, buildings — is what the funding ask actually buys; the machines themselves are ~4% of Phase 1 capex ($80 K for the entire ~150-asset fleet, batteries and spares included) — a consumable in the fullest sense.

Labor deserves the extra sentence because it is the largest line and the most commonly mismanaged in venue businesses. The staffing model (Volume 9 for front-of-house, Volume 7 for The Works) is built on cross-training: every marshal completes Artisan basic training, every Artisan can run a division's customer operation, and RC WORLD OS's role-based interfaces mean a staff member switching roles switches apps, not systems. Because telemetry supervises what human eyes once had to — battery states, geofences, session clocks — the park's staff-to-concurrent-customer ratio is materially better than attraction-industry norms, and the staff the park does employ spend their time on the two things software cannot do: hospitality and wrenching.

### 5.4 The customer journey in brief

### 5.7 Operating calendar

The park operates year-round with a season-shaped schedule: extended hours and full staffing across weekends, school holidays, and the peak season; a leaner weekday pattern built around bookings (school programs, corporate events, member sessions) rather than walk-in traffic; and one scheduled weekday closure per week in the off-season for deep maintenance — the day The Works catches up on the preventive-maintenance matrix without competing with revenue (Volume 7, Chapter 5). Evening operation arrives with Phase 3's lighting; until then, seasonal daylight sets the closing bell. Weather closures follow published thresholds per division (wind limits for aviation, standing-water limits for tracks, lightning protocol park-wide — division volumes, Safety chapters), with RC WORLD OS handling automated rebooking. The events calendar — league nights, championships, harvest festivals, corporate seasons — is planned annually and is a first-class revenue plan, not an afterthought: Volume 9, Chapter 7 carries the template calendar.

### 5.8 Safety architecture at a glance

Safety at RC WORLD is layered so that no single failure — human or technical — can produce a serious outcome. Layer one is **design**: customers operate from stations physically separated from live fields; spectator areas are barriered; the airfield is fully netted; the charging bunker is fire-isolated construction. Layer two is **telemetry**: every vehicle's RCW Node enforces geofences and the battery window automatically, and any marshal or Artisan can kill any vehicle from the app. Layer three is **procedure**: the Toolbox Talk gates first use; marshals hold defined per-zone authority; SOPs and emergency procedures (fire, injury, severe weather, lost child) are drilled per the schedules in each division volume. Layer four is **culture**: the incident and near-miss log in RC WORLD OS is blame-free and reviewed weekly, because a park that hides its near-misses schedules its accidents. The insurance program sits behind all four layers, specified in Volume 10.

**Scope.** The indoor all-weather arena (year-round revenue floor, small-scale racing and crawler courses under roof); the **RC Academy** classrooms (structured curriculum, school programs, holiday camps — the education engine at full power); the corporate event centre; night-racing lighting on Tracks A and B; and the **franchise pilot** — the first RC WORLD site operated under license, using Volume 12 and the multi-site architecture of RC WORLD OS.

**Why this scope.** Phase 3 attacks the two structural constraints of an outdoor single-site business — weather and geography. The arena and lighting extend the operating calendar into evenings and winters; the Academy converts the License program into accredited education revenue; the franchise pilot converts the operating manual you are reading into a licensable product.

**Milestones and exit criteria** (project months): arena and Academy open by ≈Month 40; night racing live by ≈Month 36; franchise pilot site selected by ≈Month 40, opened by ≈Month 54 against the Volume 12 package. Each Phase 3 element must clear a ≥25% ROIC hurdle on incremental EBITDA at the operating Month-30 review (Volume 10, §10.2). Phase 3 "exit" is the five-year review: cumulative performance per Volume 10, a validated franchise unit, and a board decision on the expansion paths in Chapter 10.

> **Investor Note.** The phase structure is an option ladder, not a treadmill. Each gate is a genuine decision point at which the board can accelerate, hold, or re-scope with current evidence. The base-case financials assume all three phases proceed on schedule; the downside case in Volume 10 shows Phase 1 standing alone as a viable, cash-generating business — smaller, but solvent.

Two construction principles apply across all phases and are binding on Volume 11's contractor packages: **no phase's construction may degrade an operating zone's revenue** (segregated construction access, dust and noise controls, and scheduling around peak trading are contractual requirements, not requests); and **every structure is designed for its next phase** (the entry pavilion's services are sized for the Phase 2 restaurant; conduit runs anticipate Phase 3 lighting; the pond's edge design anticipates the grandstand sightline). Retrofitting is the most expensive construction there is, and the master plan exists to avoid buying anything twice.

### 7.5 The pre-opening critical path

Within Phase 1 (all months here are project months), four workstreams must converge in Months 10–12, and the schedule risk lives in their interaction rather than in any one of them. **Civil and buildings** is the longest pole and the least compressible; it is contracted fixed-price with milestone payments aligned to the tranche gates. **Fleet and spares** is the shortest but has ocean freight in it: purchase orders per Volume 8 are placed by Month 2 so that vehicles arrive in Month 4–5, leaving time for RCW Node fitment, QA, and the 3-deep spares wall before staff training needs working machines — ≥80% of the fleet landed is itself a T2 condition. **Technology** must demonstrate the RC WORLD OS end-to-end beta at the T2 gate, roughly five months before opening, so the final stretch is hardening, mesh commissioning, and load testing — integration, not invention. **People** starts hiring at Month 5, trains against the volumes from Month 7, and runs the soft opening as its graduation exercise. The soft opening and presales push are the deliberate collision point where all four streams meet under low stakes, generating the evidence the T3 gate requires — soft opening complete, safety sign-off, and the first 1,000 paid Shifts sold. Grand opening follows only when the punch list is closed — an opening delayed two weeks costs less than a launch that teaches the catchment the park isn't ready.

### 8.2 Break-even and payback

Monthly operating break-even — the month from which monthly EBITDA is positive and stays positive — is modeled at **operating Month 16–19** (base model: Month 16). The ask carries the business through the top of that range with margin: the four non-capex components of Section 6.1 place **$750 K of cash at opening**, and the monthly model never breaches the **$250 K minimum-cash policy floor** (Volume 10, §6.4). Full payback occurs in **Year 4–5** — the base model crosses at **Month ~53** on Phase 1 capital; the ambitious case pulls payback into Year 4. The sensitivity drivers, in order of impact: Year-1 demand capture, average revenue per visitor, and the Phase 2 opening date; Volume 10, Chapter 9 quantifies each.

### 8.3 What makes the margin

Steady-state EBITDA of 24–28% is a *structural* outcome of decisions documented elsewhere in this plan, and diligence should test those decisions rather than the percentage: the near-zero marginal cost of an incremental Shift (Section 4.3); a maintenance line engineered down by the electromechanical fleet doctrine and the spares-and-rebuild program (Volume 7); telemetry that prevents the battery and asset losses that quietly bleed rental fleets; labor productivity from RC WORLD OS automation (one Artisan supervises multiple concurrent Shifts because the software watches every battery); and a revenue mix that shifts toward high-margin memberships, events, and sponsorship as the park matures.

### 8.4 Funding recap

$2.6 M ask; three tranches ($1.2 M / $850 K / $550 K) gated per Section 6.3; Phases 2–3 funded from operating cash flow, with Phase 2 backed by an optional $600 K equipment-and-fit-out facility analyzed, but not required, in Volume 10, §3.5; five-year total capex ≈$3.9 M.

### 8.6 Sensitivity headlines

Volume 10, Chapter 9 carries the full sensitivity and scenario analysis; the shape of it belongs here. The model is **most sensitive to Year-1 demand capture**: a ramp 25% below base pushes break-even toward the far end of the operating Month 16–19 band and consumes much of the operating funding and reserve (which is what they exist for), while the ambitious case pulls Phase-1 payback to Month ~44 (Year 4). It is **moderately sensitive to average revenue per visitor** (the F&B and retail capture assumptions are deliberately modest, so the risk is asymmetric to the upside) and to the **Phase 2 opening date** (a six-month slip defers, but does not destroy, the Year-3 revenue step). It is **notably insensitive** to the variables outsiders usually worry about: fleet cost inflation (the fleet is 4.3% of capex and wholesale prices have been deflating for a decade), battery costs, and energy prices. The downside scenario — Phase 1 standing alone, Phases 2–3 never built — models as a smaller but solvent business, which most distinguishes this plan from attraction developments whose economics only work at full build-out.

## 10. Expansion Roadmap

### 10.1 The five-year growth logic

Growth follows a strict sequence: **prove the unit, extend the calendar, then replicate.** Years 1–2 prove Phase 1 unit economics. Years 2–3 extend the revenue calendar (Phase 2 divisions, restaurant, events). Years 3–5 attack weather and geography (arena, night racing) and convert the format into products that scale beyond one site: the Academy curriculum, the franchise package, and the technology platform. Nothing in the roadmap requires capital beyond the ≈$3.9 M five-year total; everything in Sections 10.2–10.4 is expansion *option*, exercised only on evidence.

### 10.2 Growth within the site

- **Indoor all-weather arena (Phase 3, canonical).** Small-scale racing and crawler courses under roof; converts winter and rain days from zero-revenue to base-load; doubles as the corporate and party venue in bad weather.
- **RC Academy (Phase 3, canonical).** Classroom-based structured curriculum: school STEM programs, holiday camps, adult engineering courses built on Volume 7's custom-build instruction. Education is the park's highest-defensibility revenue — timetabled, prepaid, weekday, and grant-eligible — and the Academy formalizes the License program's top tiers into certification.
- **Night racing (Phase 3, canonical).** Lighting on Tracks A and B opens evening sessions, league nights, and a summer race-series product aimed at the adult hobbyist segment.
- **Event franchise formats.** Recurring owned events — endurance races, mining production championships, drift nights, harvest festivals — each a marketing beacon and a high-margin revenue day (event and operations chapters of Volumes 3–6; format calendar in Volume 9).

### 10.3 Growth beyond the site

- **Second site (Years 4–5 option).** A company-owned second park in a comparable city, using Volume 11's design package and Volume 8's supply chain at meaningfully lower cost and risk than site one — the construction drawings, supplier relationships, OS platform, and trained-manager pipeline all exist, and the second site's pre-opening marketing starts with a License-holding diaspora rather than a cold audience. Site two also answers the strategic question franchising raises: whether replication economics are better captured as owned EBITDA or as royalties. The plan's position is to run both experiments — one owned site, one franchised pilot — and let five-year evidence decide the mix. Decision gate: site-one performance at or above the Volume 10 base case for four consecutive quarters.
- **Franchise pilot (Phase 3, canonical) and system (Year 5+).** The pilot validates Volume 12's package: site selection, localization method, fee structure, training, and brand standards, on the multi-site RC WORLD OS architecture (Volume 13) that gives the franchisor live telemetry-level visibility into every franchised park — an oversight capability no conventional venue franchisor has. Success converts RC WORLD's economics from single-site EBITDA to royalty streams.
- **Compact format ("RC WORLD Arena", exploratory).** An indoor-only urban format derived from the Phase 3 arena learnings — one to two divisions, small footprint, mall-compatible. Deliberately unmodeled; noted here so the franchise architecture keeps it possible.

### 10.4 Platform and product options

The technology built for the park has standalone option value, carried at zero in the model: the **RCW Node** as a product for RC clubs, schools, and competing venues (sold or licensed with the fleet-management layer of RC WORLD OS); the **Academy curriculum** as licensable STEM content; and the **License program** as a portable credential across the future site network — the loyalty asset that makes each new site open with an installed base.

### 10.5 Strategic partnerships and sponsorship

The sponsorship strategy (detailed in Volume 9, with the partner-market context in Volume 2) targets four partner classes, each with a natural asset to sponsor: **RC manufacturers and distributors** (fleet supply partnerships, liveried vehicle classes, "powered by" zone naming — suppliers gain the world's most visible product demonstration venue); **construction, mining, and agricultural equipment brands** (zone naming for the Mining and Agriculture Zones — miniature versions of their real-world machines are marketing they cannot buy elsewhere); **regional consumer brands** (event-series title sponsorship, grandstand and lawn naming from Phase 2); and **education partners** (schools, STEM bodies, vocational institutions channeling Academy demand). Sponsorship is modeled conservatively in Volume 10 and treated as margin upside; the strategic value — credibility, fleet cost offsets, and marketing reach — exceeds the revenue line.

Two disciplines govern the pipeline. First, **sponsorship follows audience**: serious approaches to zone-naming and title partners begin after the park can show verified attendance and social reach from a full season — sponsors bought early are sponsors bought cheap. Second, **no sponsorship may bend doctrine**: a fleet-supply partnership that required non-standard batteries, a non-parity vehicle class, or hydraulics in rental duty would fail the doctrine test regardless of its value. Supplier partnerships are structured instead around what RC WORLD genuinely offers manufacturers — the highest-duty-cycle public demonstration of their products in the world, and telemetry-backed durability data no factory test rig can replicate (a data asset Volume 8 develops into negotiating leverage).
