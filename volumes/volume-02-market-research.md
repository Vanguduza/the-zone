## 1. Research Methodology & Sources

### 1.1 How to read this volume

Every factual claim in this volume falls into one of two classes. The first class is **verifiable public fact**: a company exists, was founded in a given year, sells a given product at a given listed price, operates a venue at a given address. These we state plainly and, where prices or programs change frequently (as attraction pricing does), we date them. The second class is **estimate**: market sizes, growth rates, market shares, willingness-to-pay. For these, no single authoritative number exists — the RC industry has no equivalent of automotive registration data — so we state ranges and attribute them to a source category rather than pretending one analyst's figure is truth.

The reader should also understand what this volume is *not*. It is not a purchased syndicated market report; it is a synthesis of publicly available research, manufacturer and venue primary sources, hobby-trade press, and the founder's own operating knowledge of the industry, assembled in mid-2026. Where the public record is thin — and for some questions, notably Chinese factory revenues and the finances of small RC venues, it is very thin — we say so rather than fabricate.

### 1.2 Data confidence levels

Every table and material claim in this volume carries an implicit confidence grade. Where the grade matters to a decision, we state it explicitly using this scale:

| Grade | Meaning | Typical sources | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Verified fact** | Directly confirmed from primary sources; stable | Company registries, venue price pages, manufacturer sites | Dig This Las Vegas sells a "Mega Dig" combo from $595 (July 2026 pricing) |
| **B — Corroborated estimate** | Multiple independent sources agree within a reasonable band | 2+ analyst firms, trade press, distributor catalogues | Global RC car market $1.5–6.2 B (2025) depending on scope |
| **C — Single-source or inferred** | One credible source, or triangulated from adjacent data | One analyst report, one interview, one forum consensus | Market-share estimates for individual manufacturers |
| **D — Founder judgment / assumption** | No reliable external data; stated as an assumption to be tested | Operating experience, first-principles reasoning | Visit-frequency assumptions in Chapter 9 |

> **Investor Note.** Treat Grade C and D items as hypotheses with a test plan, not facts. Every Grade D assumption that materially drives the financial model is flagged in Chapter 9 and carried into the sensitivity analysis in Volume 10, Chapter 8. If a number in this plan ever hardens from D to A — for example, once the park has twelve months of real visit-frequency data — the relevant volumes get a revision.

### 1.3 Source categories used in this volume

- **Industry analysts (2025–2026)** — commercial market-research publishers covering RC vehicles, remote-control toys, hobby goods, and family entertainment centers. Their scopes differ wildly (toy-grade in or out; drones in or out; parts and batteries in or out), which is precisely why we quote ranges.
- **Manufacturer and distributor primary sources** — company websites, investor pages, catalogues, and trade-fair listings (e.g., Intermodellbau Dortmund exhibitor lists, China Toy Expo profiles).
- **Venue primary sources** — published price lists and booking pages of the competitor venues analysed in Chapter 8, captured June–July 2026.
- **Hobby trade press and community** — RC-specialist media, club forums (e.g., the German Modell-Baustelle and Modelltruckforum communities), and event reporting. Used for culture, practice, and precedent; never for financial figures.
- **Attractions-industry bodies** — location-based-entertainment industry reporting (IAAPA-category data as republished by analysts) for the demand-side market in Chapter 9.

### 1.4 The honesty rule

Three commitments govern everything that follows. First: **no fabricated precision.** If the best available answer is "somewhere between $1.5 B and $6.2 B depending on what you count," that is the answer we print. Second: **no fake citations.** Companies, products, venues, and prices named in this volume are real and were verified against primary sources during drafting; estimates are labelled as estimates. Third: **negative results are reported.** Where we searched for something and found little — for instance, ready-made 1/14 scale human figures at fleet quantities (Section 7.5), or audited financials for any RC venue anywhere (Section 8.7) — the gap is itself a finding, and we record it.

## 2. The Global RC Industry

### 2.1 Market size: the honest range

Anyone who reads three market reports on radio control comes away with three different numbers, because the category has no agreed definition. Some analysts count only hobby-grade cars sold through hobby channels; others fold in toy-grade product in mass retail; others include or exclude drones, aircraft, boats, parts, batteries, and radios. The defensible statement is a range (Grade B):

- **RC cars and trucks (ground vehicles):** industry analysts (2025–2026) place the global market between **$1.5 B and $6.2 B (2025)**, growing at roughly **5.8–7.5% CAGR** into the early 2030s. The low end reflects narrow hobby-grade-only scopes; the high end sweeps in toy-grade product and aftermarket. At least one narrow-scope analyst publishes a figure below $1 B and a growth rate near 11% — an outlier that illustrates the scope problem rather than a contradiction.
- **Hobby radio control overall** (cars, aircraft, boats, radios, parts, batteries; excluding commercial drones): approximately **$3.2–7.0 B**, growing **6–7.5%** annually (industry analysts, 2025–2026).
- **Segment structure:** analysts consistently report ground vehicles as the largest hobby-RC category, hobby-grade product at roughly **45–57% of RC-car market value** and rising, and toy-grade at roughly **30–34% and declining in share** through 2034 (Grade B on direction, C on the specific percentages).

> **Investor Note.** RC WORLD does not need the market to be $6 B rather than $1.5 B. The park sells *location-based entertainment*, not RC products; the industry figures matter because they prove three things that hold at either end of the range — the equipment supply chain is deep, mature, and brutally price-competitive; a large global hobbyist population exists to anchor community and events; and the category is growing, not shrinking.

### 2.2 The toy-grade / hobby-grade bifurcation

The single most important structural trend, reported consistently across analyst firms and trade sources (2025–2026), is **bifurcation**: mass-market toy-grade RC (blister-pack, non-serviceable, sub-$80, sold on gifting occasions) is stagnant to declining in share, while hobby-grade (serviceable, tunable, LiPo-powered, with spare-parts ecosystems) grows in both units and value. Trade-platform data published by a major Chinese e-commerce group in 2026 described the low-end segment as "collapsing under the weight of its own commoditization" while the hobby-grade segment flourishes on adult enthusiast demand.

The driver is the collapse in hobby-grade entry pricing. A genuinely fast, repairable, brushless-optioned 1/14 buggy now retails for $60–120 — a price point that did not exist in 2015 and which Chinese manufacturers (Chapter 3) created. This trend is the industry handing RC WORLD its fleet doctrine: the park's entire operating model (Volume 1; Volume 8) is built on cheap, durable, repairable hobby-grade equipment bought at wholesale.

> **Field Note.** How to read RC market reports without being fooled: check three scope questions before trusting any figure — Is toy-grade included? Are drones included? Are parts, batteries, and radios included? The $897 M and $6.2 B "RC car market" figures both circulating in 2025–2026 are not contradictory; they answer differently on all three questions. Any pitch deck (including ours) quoting a single unqualified number has either not read the reports or hopes you haven't.

Three further structural observations matter more than any headline number:

1. **Fragmentation at every level.** No manufacturer holds a dominant global share. The strongest brands (Traxxas, the Horizon Hobby portfolio, Tamiya) are strong regionally and by category; analyst share estimates for the leader alone range from ~14% to ~22% depending on scope (Grade C). Hundreds of Chinese factories and a long tail of specialists share the rest. Fragmentation is a gift to a fleet buyer: genuine price competition, substitutable suppliers for nearly every asset class, no single point of supply failure.
2. **Equipment-rich, venue-poor.** The industry ships millions of vehicles a year into a world with remarkably few purpose-built places to run them. Most hobbyists drive in car parks and back gardens; club tracks are volunteer-run and often intimidating to novices; almost nothing exists for a family that wants to *try* RC without first buying it. That supply–demand gap between equipment and venues is the white space RC WORLD occupies (Chapter 10).
3. **Asia-Pacific leads, everywhere grows.** Analysts consistently put Asia-Pacific at the largest regional revenue share of the RC market (roughly 35–40%, Grade C) with North America and Europe close behind — meaning the concept is not hostage to one region's taste, which matters for the franchise thesis (Volume 12).

### 2.4 Distribution: from hobby shop to checkout cart

The traditional three-tier chain (factory → national distributor → local hobby shop) is eroding. Analysts and trade press (2025–2026) report steady share shift toward e-commerce: brand-direct webstores, specialist e-tailers, and direct-from-China marketplaces. Local hobby shops survive where they add community — a track, a workshop counter, race nights — which is exactly the bundle RC WORLD industrializes. Two consequences:

- **For procurement:** the park buys at or near factory prices through the same channels hobbyists already trust; the wholesale relationships specified in Volume 8 are conventional practice at unusual volume, not a special favor to be negotiated.
- **For retail strategy:** the park's shop (Volume 9) should not attempt to beat online prices on commodity parts. It wins on immediacy (broken part, fixed today), curation (the exact spares for the park's own fleet classes), and impulse/gift purchases at the exit.

### 2.6 Regional split

Analyst regional breakdowns differ in detail but agree on shape (Grade B): **Asia-Pacific** holds the largest share of RC product revenue (roughly 35–40%), driven by both manufacturing-country domestic demand and Japan's mature hobby base; **North America** is the largest single national market with the strongest premium-RTR culture; **Europe** is comparable in aggregate to North America with a stronger kit/scale culture and more fragmented distribution. Growth skews to Asia-Pacific and to emerging middle-income markets. For RC WORLD the read is simple: the concept's supply chain, culture references, and demand precedents all exist in every candidate region for the reference site and for later franchises — no region is disqualified on market-structure grounds, and Volume 12's franchise screening therefore turns on city-level variables (catchment, land, climate) rather than country-level RC penetration.

### 2.7 Adjacent categories that matter

Three adjacent markets bear on the plan. **Consumer drones** are an order of magnitude larger than hobby RC and culturally separate, but FPV racing (Section 8.4) supplies proven arena formats. **Static and diecast scale models** form a multi-billion-dollar collector market that feeds the scale-realism trend and overlaps RC WORLD's retail assortment. **STEM/robotics education** is a fast-growing purchasing category for schools — the wedge for the schools segment in Chapter 9 and the RC Academy in Phase 3.

## 3. China — the Manufacturing Engine

### 3.1 The geography: two clusters and a delta

Chinese RC manufacturing concentrates in Guangdong province, in two distinguishable clusters plus a supporting delta-wide ecosystem:

- **Shantou / Chenghai district** — historically "China's toy capital." Toy and RC manufacturing here grew out of the plastic-toy industry: Chenghai hosts thousands of toy factories and the RC brands that graduated from them. Huina, MJX (Meijiaxin), Double Eagle, and WLtoys all have Shantou-area roots. The cluster's DNA is high-volume injection molding, aggressive cost engineering, and fast imitation — and, over the last decade, a genuine climb up the quality ladder.
- **Shenzhen / Dongguan / Pearl River Delta** — the electronics cluster. Radio systems (Flysky, Radiolink), ESCs and motors (Hobbywing, Surpass Hobby), batteries (Grepow/Gens Ace and the direct-to-consumer LiPo houses such as CNHL), and every PCB, sensor, and connector the park's own RCW Node needs (JLCPCB-class fabrication; Volume 13). Shenzhen's advantage is not cheap labor but component density: any electronic subassembly can be sourced, prototyped, and revised within days.
- **Foshan and the wider delta** — precision metal work. The high-end 1/14 hydraulic and machined-metal model makers (LESU in Foshan's Nanhai district; JDModel and Kabolite's premium lines) sit closer to the CNC and hydraulics supply base than to the toy cluster.

### 3.4 OEM/ODM culture: how the cluster actually works

Western buyers routinely misread the Chinese RC industry by assuming brand = factory. The operative culture is **OEM/ODM**: a factory tools a design and sells it under its own brand, under other brands, in white-box form, and in tweaked variants to whoever orders a container. The same excavator casting may appear under three names at three prices; a European distributor's "own brand" (Chapter 5: Amewi's AMEWI-branded construction line, Carson's licensed models produced with Double Eagle) is frequently a Chenghai or Shantou product with different stickers, packaging, and — critically — different quality-control gates.

For RC WORLD this culture is an asset with three handles:

1. **Price discovery.** Because identical hardware ships under multiple labels, the park can benchmark true factory cost and negotiate wholesale within 10–20% of it (procurement method, Volume 8, Chapter 3).
2. **Fleet customization.** ODM factories accept paint, sticker, and spec changes at modest MOQs (typically 50–300 units for cosmetic changes, Grade C). A park ordering 24 identical touring cars is a small but real ODM customer, and park-liveried fleets are achievable from the first container.
3. **Second-sourcing.** The 1:3 excavator-to-dumper doctrine fails if dumpers go out of stock. Every fleet class in Volume 8 carries a named second source precisely because the OEM/ODM web makes near-equivalents available.

> **Field Note.** The cluster's weakness is documentation and revision control. A "V4" machine may quietly ship with a different ESC than last quarter's V4. The park's receiving SOP (Volume 8, Chapter 6) therefore teardown-inspects the first unit of every batch and logs component-level deltas in RC WORLD OS before a batch enters service.

The same badge covers a 175× price range. This is why the plan's fleet doctrine (source digest; Volume 4) is precise about *which rung* serves which purpose: electromechanical machines rent to the public because Artisans can rebuild a lead-screw actuator in minutes and there is no fluid to leak into the sand; hydraulics are reserved for supervised premium experiences and display, where their realism justifies their fragility and price. A competitor who buys the wrong rung — hydraulics for rental, or toys for enthusiasts — bleeds maintenance hours or credibility respectively; this single procurement judgment is worth more than any marketing budget, and it is the kind of encoded know-how Section 10.2 counts as defensibility.

### 3.6 Trade channels and how the park buys

Practical sourcing routes, in ascending order of commitment: (1) **retail-adjacent e-tail** (AliExpress/Banggood-class, brand stores) for samples and urgent spares; (2) **B2B platforms** (Alibaba, and for those with mainland purchasing capability, 1688.com at domestic-China prices) for case-quantity orders and ODM conversations; (3) **direct factory accounts** with pro-forma invoicing and freight forwarding for container-scale fleet buys; (4) **trade fairs** (Canton Fair; China Toy Expo, where Double Eagle exhibits) for relationship-building and new-product scouting. The park's procurement SOPs (Volume 8, Chapters 3–5) formalize the ladder; the point for this volume is that every route is mature, English-transactable, and used daily by thousands of Western buyers — China sourcing is a discipline, not an adventure.

### 3.7 What China means for the plan

China is not merely where RC WORLD buys machines; it is why the business model works at all. The Phase 1 fleet of ~150 powered assets is budgeted (Volume 10) at a small fraction of what the same capability would have cost in 2010, and the parts stream that keeps utilization high is a commodity market with published prices. The strategic risks — tariff exposure, quality drift, IP ambiguity, single-region concentration — are real and carried in the risk register (Volume 1, Chapter 8; Volume 8, Chapter 2), but they are managed procurement risks, not existential ones.

### 4.2 Drift culture and the Mini-Z lesson

Japan invented RC drift as a discipline: rear-wheel-drive chassis, spec low-grip tires, judged style rather than raw lap time, tight indoor tracks with polished surfaces, and a social scene closer to a skate park than a race club. The discipline exports superbly — the leading US drift venue (Section 8.2) runs Japanese P-tile flooring and Japanese-designed chassis — and it is tailor-made for a park: low speeds, high skill ceiling, spectacular to watch, gentle on equipment.

Kyosho's **Mini-Z** (1/28 scale, launched 1999) proved a second venue-relevant thesis: shrink the car and the venue shrinks with it. A 40-foot Mini-Z track fits where no 1/10 track can, rentals run ~$20/hour retail (Section 8.2), and the format is weatherproof. RC WORLD's Phase 3 indoor arena (Volume 11) carries a Mini-Z/1/28 layout for exactly this reason.

### 4.3 What RC WORLD learns from Japanese RC retail and circuit culture

Japan is the only country where major manufacturers operate destination circuits and experience retail at scale, and the lessons are direct:

- **Tamiya's Kakegawa Circuit** (Shizuoka): a 290 m outdoor on-road circuit — among the world's largest — plus off-road course and rock-crawling field, run on a membership basis, **Tamiya equipment only**, with amenities up to a women-only lounge. Lessons: single-marque control simplifies marshalling and parity (RC WORLD achieves the same via a standardized rental fleet); membership converts a track into a community; comfort amenities widen the demographic.
- **Kyosho Village** (Tokyo, opened 2022) and Kyosho shop-in-shop corners with free-to-run Mini-Z tracks: retail wrapped around a try-it-now experience. Lesson: the track sells the product; the product sells the track. RC WORLD's retail counter sits in sight of the tracks for the same reason (Volume 11, Chapter 5).
- **Circuit etiquette culture**: posted rules, staffed operation on weekends, tire and equipment standards enforced without apology. Lesson: firm technical rules read as professionalism, not unfriendliness, when the staff are helpful — the model for RC WORLD's marshal doctrine (Volume 9, Chapter 4).

> **Field Note.** Japanese RC retail treats beginners as future experts, not walk-in revenue: free beginner tracks, staffed advice, visible progression ladders. RC WORLD's License program (Volume 9) is that ladder, industrialized.

### 4.4 The kit-culture lesson and its limits

Japan's RC market is distinctive in one more way: it never abandoned the **kit**. Tamiya's business remains anchored in boxes of parts — the build is half the product — and the TT-02 chassis has introduced hundreds of thousands of people to RC assembly. The transferable insight is that *making* deepens attachment: a customer who has assembled or tuned something returns more often and spends more. The limit of the lesson is throughput — a park cannot ask walk-in customers to build before driving. RC WORLD's translation is layered: rentals are RTR by definition; the pit-stop battery swap and Tow-Truck Protocol give casual customers a taste of the mechanical world; The Works offers watch-the-Artisan repair theatre; and the Phase 3 Academy sells the full build experience (Volume 7's custom-build curriculum) to the minority who want it. Japan proves that minority is loyal beyond its size.

One further Japanese datapoint for the record: Japanese manufacturers hold significant global share by analyst estimates (Tamiya alone variously estimated at 6–12% of RC-car revenue, Kyosho at high single digits — Grade C, scope-dependent), and their products command premium pricing on quality reputation. For fleet procurement this cashes out narrowly: the park buys Japanese where reliability is safety-critical or parts standardization pays (Futaba/Sanwa radios for staff use; MST drift platforms per canon), and Chinese everywhere the 3:1-spares doctrine absorbs failure cheaply.

RC WORLD's procurement policy of multi-sourcing and modest inventory depth (Volume 8) exists partly because supplier failure is a normal industry event.

### 5.2 German scale-truck culture: the deepest precedent for the Construction Division

Germany hosts the world's most developed **Funktionsmodellbau** (functional model-building) culture: 1/14 and 1/16 trucks and construction machines built to near-industrial standards and operated on elaborate model construction sites ("Parcours"). Key elements, all verified against 2025–2026 sources:

- **Manufacturers:** **Wedico** (the legacy name that defined premium 1/16 German truck models; today primarily a heritage/parts marque — Grade B), **ScaleART** (Gaggenau-area manufacturer of made-in-Germany 1/14 trucks with 15-bar hydraulics, sold RTR or as kits), **Servonaut/tematik** (electronics for scale trucks), **Pistenking**, **Veroma**, and Chinese premium imports — above all **LESU** — which now dominate the value end of the same culture.
- **Clubs and venues:** permanent club-run indoor and outdoor Parcours across the German-speaking world — the **Stonebreaker Area** (Lower Saxony), Indoor-Parcours Rain am Lech (Bavaria), Modellbaustelle Süddeutschland (Ehingen), RCTKA Karlsruhe, and dozens more organized through the Modell-Baustelle and Modelltruckforum communities. These are club facilities, not commercial attractions — members build the diorama, then drive on it.
- **Events:** **Intermodellbau Dortmund** — the world's largest model-making fair, running since 1978; the 2026 edition (16–19 April) featured the mini-Truck-Club Recklinghausen's 40th-anniversary display: a **345 m² model landscape** with bridges and tunnels plus an **81 m² public action Parcours** issuing free "mini trucker licenses" to young visitors.

> **Field Note.** The German clubs prove the *demand* for exactly what RC WORLD's Construction Division sells — hours of absorbed, purposeful operation of miniature machines in a believable world — and simultaneously prove the *gap*: access today requires club membership, your own $1,000+ machine, and volunteer labor. Nobody in Europe has industrialized it into a ticketed attraction. The Recklinghausen club's "mini trucker license" for children is, in embryo, the RC WORLD License program.

### 5.3 European events and channels worth tracking

Beyond Intermodellbau, the European calendar (model fairs in Nuremberg — the Spielwarenmesse toy fair where Amewi and Carson exhibit; regional truck meets; the EOS/ETS racing series where LRP-class electronics compete) is the park's radar for product trends and a future marketing channel for a European franchise. European distribution partners (Amewi-class wholesalers) become relevant the moment a franchise territory needs EU-warehoused spares with EU warranty law compliance (Volume 12, Chapter 6).

### 5.4 What the German Parcours teach about diorama engineering

Because Chapter 7 will send the park shopping for miniature scenery, it is worth recording what the German club builders — the world's most experienced miniature-worksite engineers — have converged on after decades of trial (community consensus from the Modell-Baustelle/Modelltruckforum record, Grade B):

- **Substrate is everything.** Successful Parcours run compacted fine gravel and sharp sand over drainage, not loose play sand; loose sand ingests into gearboxes and bogs wheeled machines. The canon aggregate specification (loose sand to 10 mm crushed stone in graded zones) matches this learning.
- **Timber-and-steel infrastructure, scenic skin.** Bridges, quay walls, and ramps are built structurally (plywood, steel section) and then dressed to scale — the reverse (scenic material asked to bear load) fails within weeks.
- **Grades kill motors.** Club haul roads stay shallow; the canon 15° maximum incline encodes the same physics.
- **Water is the highest-maintenance feature per square meter** — but also the highest-engagement. Clubs that added water crossings and wash bays report disproportionate dwell time; the Marine Division and Track C water hazards inherit both the warning and the promise.
- **Staging beats sprawl.** The most-photographed Parcours are dense vignettes (a weighbridge, a fuel point, a site office with lit windows) connected by working roads — not uniform detail everywhere. RC WORLD's theming budget (Volume 11, Chapter 7) concentrates on vignette nodes for the same reason.

The strategic conclusion repeats Section 5.2's: this culture has perfected the *product* RC WORLD needs and never attempted the *business*. Hiring or contracting from this community — German and Dutch club builders take commissions — is a credible route to Phase 1 theming quality that no local landscaping contractor will match unaided.

Read across: even the industry's biggest players run thin margins on hardware, and *community events are the first casualty of a hardware-margin squeeze*. A venue business whose revenue **is** the event does not face that trade-off — it is the trade-off, solved.

### 6.2 RC park precedents on US soil

The US has thousands of RC tracks but almost no RC *parks* in the RC WORLD sense. The closest precedents:

- **Club and commercial tracks** — hundreds of volunteer club circuits plus a thin layer of commercial operations (SDRC Raceway, Nor-Cal Hobbies — analysed with prices in Section 8.2/8.3).
- **Hobby-shop/track hybrids** — the dominant surviving retail format: a shop financing a track that feeds the shop.
- **Full-size dig attractions** — Dig This (Las Vegas) and Diggerland USA (New Jersey), which prove the construction-play demand thesis at full scale (Section 8.6).
- **Crawler parks** — informal rock-crawling courses at hobby shops and public parks; a handful of paid crawler courses attached to attractions.

The absence is the finding: **no US operator combines racing, construction, crawling, marine, and aviation in one ticketed multi-zone venue.** The format RC WORLD proposes does not exist there yet (Grade A as of July 2026 research; Section 8.1 covers the nearest global analogues).

### 6.3 The SEMA-style aftermarket lesson

American RC replicates the American car-culture economy in miniature: the vehicle is the entry ticket, the aftermarket is the lifestyle. Pro-Line built a durable business purely on tires, wheels, and bodies; entire YouTube genres exist around upgrading a $300 truck with $600 of parts. For RC WORLD this validates two revenue lines: **customization retail** (bodies, wheels, lights fitted at The Works while the customer watches — Volume 9, Chapter 6) and **build-your-own programs** (Volume 7's custom-build tutorials as paid Academy courses in Phase 3). The park should treat the aftermarket impulse — *make it mine* — as a first-class monetization mechanic, not an afterthought.

### 6.4 Traxxas as the customer-experience benchmark

Traxxas deserves a section of its own, not as a supplier (the park's fleet doctrine buys mostly Chinese) but as the industry's best case study in **removing friction from a technical hobby**. Its playbook, assembled from public record: ready-to-run as the default (batteries and radio in the box, decades before rivals); waterproof electronics as standard, ending the hobby's oldest anxiety; a self-righting monster-truck feature so novices never walk to flip a truck; plain-language support and generous service; dealer-network merchandising that looks like power tools, not toys; and marketing through full-size motorsport sponsorship rather than hobby channels. The result is analyst-estimated category leadership (Section 6.1) at premium prices.

Every element maps to a park decision: RTR philosophy → rental-first fleet; waterproofing → all-weather operating envelope specs (Volume 7); self-righting → the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol solving the same "dead vehicle, embarrassed customer" moment with gameplay; plain-language support → Artisan hosting culture and the Toolbox Talk; motorsport aesthetics → the park's pit lane, grandstand, and livery standards (Volume 11). Traxxas proved that the mass market wants RC *minus its frictions*; RC WORLD extends the same logic one step further — RC minus ownership itself.

### 6.5 US market data points for the record

For calibration (Grade C, analyst estimates 2025–2026): North America is generally credited with roughly 30–35% of global RC-car revenue; US hobby distribution has consolidated around Horizon's house channels, AMain-class e-tailers, and a shrinking independent-shop base; and US analyst breakdowns consistently show crawling/trail and bashing growing while traditional club racing participation is flat — a hint that *experience-seeking* (trails, jumps, social video) is outgrowing *competition-seeking*, which favors a park built around experiences with competition as one layer rather than the reverse.

## 7. Diorama & Miniature-Environment Suppliers (Special Study)

*This chapter fulfils the founder's explicit commission: research Chinese diorama suppliers and manufacturers, including makers of miniature equipment replicating real work environments. It maps where a park buys convincing miniature industrial scenery at scale, and grades the confidence of each finding.*

### 7.1 Why this supply chain is strategic

RC WORLD's differentiation is not vehicles — anyone can buy a Huina excavator — it is the **world**: a mining pit that reads as a mine, a farm that reads as a farm, haul roads with real signage, scaffolding, containers, barriers, and figures at coherent scale. That world is a procurement problem in three layers: (1) **miniature work-environment equipment** at 1/14–1/16 scale; (2) **built environment** — structures, buildings, large scenic elements; (3) **terrain and scenery materials** in bulk. Each layer has a different supplier landscape, and the honest finding up front is that **no single "diorama industry" exists to order from**: the park assembles its world from four adjacent industries — the 1/14 truck-accessory ecosystem, architectural model-making, model-railway scenery manufacturing, and general Chinese contract fabrication — plus in-house work by The Works.

### 7.2 Layer 1 — 1/14 site accessories and miniature work equipment

The Works owns this pipeline; Volume 7, Chapter 8 carries the build standards and Volume 8 the sourcing SOP.

### 7.3 Layer 2 — architectural model-making firms

China has a mature **sand-table / architectural model industry** serving real-estate marketing, urban planning, and museum exhibits, concentrated in Shenzhen and Guangzhou:

- **Shenzhen QZY Models Design Co., Ltd.** (founded 2013) — custom architectural and industrial scale models; materials ABS, acrylic, metal; techniques from laser cutting and CNC to electroplating; MOQ of one model; export crating and international branches. Verified 2026 (Grade A).
- **Shenzhen Lvjian ("Green Building") Model Design** — 25 years in sand-table models: industrial plants, urban planning, multimedia/automated models. Verified 2026 (Grade A).
- **Shenzhen Haojing Model Co.** (Guangzhou-founded 2006) — architectural and landscape detail models with in-house laser and precision engraving. Verified 2026 (Grade A).
- Dozens of peer firms across the delta; the industry norm is fully custom work quoted from CAD, with 7–30 day lead times on medium pieces.

**Fit for RC WORLD:** these firms build *display-grade* miniatures — a park entrance centerpiece, the Phase 2 "master plan" souvenir model, museum-quality structures for indoor areas. They are generally **not** the right source for outdoor, driven-on, weatherproof scenery: their materials (acrylic, ABS sheet) and pricing (skilled-labor-intensive one-offs) do not suit a mining pit that takes daily hits from 5 kg trucks. The park's outdoor built environment is instead a **construction trade job at small scale** — rendered blockwork, cast concrete, treated timber, GRP — specified in Volume 11, Chapter 7, with the model firms supplying only the crown jewels.

> **Investor Note.** This split matters to capex credibility. Volume 11 budgets outdoor theming as landscaping/light-construction cost per square meter, not as "model making" — an order-of-magnitude cheaper and far more durable. The architectural-model line item in Phase 1 capex is deliberately small (display pieces only).

### 7.4 Layer 3 — scenery and terrain materials at scale

The model-railway industry solved miniature landscapes decades ago, and its supply chain now has a strong Chinese tier:

- **Chinese manufacturers/brands (Evemodel-class):** trees, painted figures, fencing, containers, building kits, benches, lamps — shipped worldwide via brand stores and marketplaces at a fraction of European brand pricing; customization accepted on items like containers (verified 2026, Grade A). Ideal for indoor dioramas, the Agriculture Zone's crop rows and farmstead details, and spectator-side scenic dressing.
- **European reference brands (Faller, Noch, Viessmann — all Intermodellbau exhibitors):** the quality benchmark for scenic materials; used selectively where longevity or realism justifies the price.
- **Bulk terrain reality check:** at 4.8 ha, the park's "terrain materials" are overwhelmingly **real civil materials** — graded aggregates (loose sand to 10 mm crushed stone per the canon mining spec), soils, turf, and plantings — procured as landscaping supplies, not hobby goods. Hobby-grade scatter and static grass are for enclosed dioramas and photo zones only; outdoors they are consumables that weather away.

### 7.5 The figures problem — an honest negative result

Convincing human figures at 1/14–1/16 anchor scale realism (a mining pit with no crew reads as a toy). Research finding (Grade A on the gap): supply is **thin and fragmented**. Model-railway figures stop at ~1/43–1/32 for painted bulk packs; 1/14 truck-scene figures exist (driver figures, a few worker poses) but at single-figure hobby prices with limited pose variety; 1/18 action-figure and garage-kit ecosystems overlap usably but off-scale. Conclusion carried into Volume 7: the park **prints and paints its own figure library** (purchased and commissioned STLs, resin/FDM, batch-painted by Artisans in workshop downtime), buying commercial figures only for hero close-up positions. This converts a supply gap into a small, ownable craft capability — and printed figures are replaceable at pennies when the inevitable excavator strike occurs.

## 8. Competitor & Precedent Analysis

*Method note: prices and program details below were captured from venue primary sources in June–July 2026 and are Grade A at capture; attraction pricing changes seasonally, so treat figures as anchors, not quotes. Financial performance of private venues is almost never published; where we infer economics, we say so.*

### 8.1 RC parks & RC theme attractions worldwide

**What exists.** The multi-zone RC park as this plan defines it — several vehicle disciplines, rental fleet, ticketed shifts, built miniature world — does not yet exist at RC WORLD's scale anywhere we could verify. What exists is a fast-thickening layer of smaller precedents:

- **Fuji Subaru Land "Heavy Machinery RC Space"** (Yamanashi, Japan; opened June 2026 at the park-adjacent Sylvans Cafe): guests operate RC excavators and a dump truck in a sandbox, hunting buried "treasure" stones; five vehicles (4 excavators + 1 dumper — note the near-1:4 ratio running opposite to our 1:3 doctrine, workable only because their dumper is decorative rather than a haulage bottleneck); **¥2,000/hour (~$13–14), shareable within a group, walk-in only**. A major Japanese theme-park operator validating RC construction as paid family entertainment is the single most encouraging datapoint of this research cycle.
- **Dig IT at Thunderbird Park** (Tamborine Mountain, Australia): a kids' mini-excavator attraction whose four zones include one literally named **"RC World"** — 1/14-scale, fully hydraulic, ~30 kg RC dozers, dumpers, excavators and loaders operated "just like on the work site"; **A$65 (~US$43) per person** admission across the four zones. Proves families pay premium prices for supervised scale-machine operation, bundled with full-size mini-diggers.
- **Dig Dig Land** (Singapore, opened late 2024): construction-themed play garden with kid-operated mini excavators, cranes, and an **RC Zone** rationed at 10 minutes per turn with requeue — a crude ancestor of the Shift and queue mechanics this plan formalizes.
- **RC Roastery** (Ramsgate, UK, opened January 2026): a café built around RC — drift track with scenery, separate adult and child construction sites, crawler course with a suspension bridge, gamified digging ("dig a kilo of gold, trade for a prize"), weekly leaderboard competitions. One founder, one retail unit; the RC-café format (already established in Thailand and Dubai, per the founder's own account) is the smallest viable expression of RC WORLD's thesis.

**Business-model reading.** All four run rental-fleet, time-rationed, staff-supervised models — i.e., they have independently converged on the same mechanics this plan specifies, at 1/50th the scope. None operates telemetry, licensing progression, or multi-division breadth; none is on more than ~0.2 ha of RC-dedicated ground.

**Lessons for RC WORLD:**
1. The demand thesis is being validated in four countries simultaneously, right now (2024–2026), by unrelated operators.
2. Every precedent is capacity-starved (walk-in queues, 10-minute rations, five-vehicle fleets) — evidence that supply, not demand, is the binding constraint. RC WORLD's 150-asset fleet and Shift system attack exactly that constraint.
3. Window of opportunity: the format is being discovered but nobody has capitalized it properly. First-mover advantage at destination scale is available and will not remain so indefinitely (see SWOT, Chapter 10).

> **Investor Note.** When a format appears independently in Japan, Australia, Singapore, and the UK within 24 months, at price points from $13/hour to $43/visit, the correct read is "category emerging, consolidation play available" — the same pattern trampoline parks showed circa 2011–2014 before chains (Sky Zone-class) industrialized them.

**Structural economics** (hobby-trade analyses, 2025–2026, Grade B): standalone indoor tracks rarely clear their rent; viability comes from hybrid revenue — retail (20–40% margin on consumables), parties/events, memberships ($50–185/month observed), coaching. Indoor carpet buildouts run $75,000–200,000+; insurance $3,000–7,000/year for track-only operations. One published back-of-envelope for a well-run indoor track: ~$210,000 annual revenue against ~$150,000 costs — a living, not a business. Trade commentary also notes stronger results in Southeast Asia (Singapore, Indonesia, Philippines), where commercial RC tracks with membership-plus-events models reportedly reach several hundred thousand dollars in annual revenue (Grade C, unaudited) — consistent with this plan's thesis that professionalized formats, not the activity itself, are the constraint in the West.

**Weaknesses of the format** (and RC WORLD's answers): single-discipline monotony (→ multi-division park); hobbyist-first culture that intimidates novices (→ rental-first fleet, Toolbox Talk induction, Artisan hosting); rentals treated as a nuisance (→ rentals are *the product*; owners' hours are the add-on, Volume 3, Chapter 8); revenue ceiling set by track count (→ ten+ simultaneous venues on one ticket).

### 8.3 Outdoor RC tracks & club circuits

The global base layer of RC infrastructure is the volunteer club circuit: mowed-and-groomed off-road tracks, oval clubs, 1/8 nitro circuits. Characteristics verified across US/UK/EU club sources: entry fees $10–30/day where charged at all; membership dues $50–200/year; working-bee labor; insurance via national federations; no rentals in most cases; opening hours hostage to volunteer availability.

- **Strengths:** authentic community; deep skills; incredibly low cost base; tolerant of weather damage.
- **Weaknesses:** no rental path for newcomers, inconsistent operation, no F&B, no families as a designed-for segment, and chronic land insecurity (clubs lose leases to development routinely).
- **Precedent value:** clubs prove that even unmarketed, amenity-free RC venues sustain decades of loyal use — the underlying activity has durable pull. Tamiya's Kakegawa membership circuit (Section 4.3) is the professionalized ceiling of this format, and the German Parcours clubs (Section 5.2) its construction-side equivalent.
- **Scale of the base:** no global census of RC tracks exists (honest gap), but national federation listings and community directories consistently indicate hundreds of active club circuits in the US and comparable density across Western Europe and Japan (Grade C). For any candidate park city, Volume 12's site-screening SOP includes a club audit within the catchment: existing clubs are simultaneously demand evidence, partner pipeline, and — if alienated — the loudest possible critics.
- **Lessons:** (1) partner, don't fight — club racers become RC WORLD's event competitors, coaches, and credibility (Volume 3, Chapter 9 sets club-night programming); (2) the park's groomed, staffed, always-open tracks are a *service upgrade* clubs cannot match; (3) offer clubs what they lack — secure venue, timing infrastructure, spectators — in exchange for what they have: skilled community.

### 8.4 Drone racing arenas

Two tiers exist, both real and current:

- **Professional/spectacle: Drone Racing League (DRL).** Twelve salaried pilots on identical spec drones racing LED courses through stadium venues (2025 Miami Invitational at loanDepot Park, ~10,000 expected spectators); acquired by Infinite Reality in April 2024 for a reported $250 M. DRL is a **media property**, not a venue business — it books stadiums per event. Lesson: drone racing is spectacular *content*; nobody has made it a sustainable *daily attraction*.
- **Grassroots: MultiGP.** The world's largest FPV network — on the order of 500 chapters and 30,000+ registered pilots (2025–2026, Grade B) — racing on temporary gate courses in fields and, increasingly, indoor venues (the 2025 championship ran indoors at Tulsa's Hardesty National BMX Hall of Fame, enabled by HDZero digital video under a metal roof). Chapters charge modest annual memberships plus small race fees.

**Reading for RC WORLD's Aviation Division (Volume 5):** the FPV community is equipment-rich and venue-poor, exactly like surface RC. A permanent netted airfield with gates, timing, charging, and spectator screens is a service no chapter can build. But FPV *rental* to novices is harder than car rental (nausea, crash rates, goggle hygiene) — hence Volume 5's tiered model: buzz-wire-style tethered and simulator introductions before rental flight, and the airfield rented to MultiGP-style chapters for events. Full-scale DRL-style spectacle is a Phase 3 event-format import, not a daily operation.

### 8.5 RC marine clubs & model boating ponds

The marine precedent is old and instructive:

- **San Francisco Model Yacht Club / Spreckels Lake** (Golden Gate Park): club founded 1898 — the oldest continuously operating model-boating body in the western hemisphere; the city built Spreckels Lake *specifically for model boating* (1902–1904) and the WPA-era clubhouse is deeded to the club. ~120–175 members, public sailing always permitted.
- **UK pond culture:** Solent RC Model Boat Club at Setley Pond (New Forest) operates on a Forestry permit with paid annual rental, insurance, and risk assessments, sharing a public pond. Colwyn Bay MBC took over management of Eirias Park's 1935 boating lake from the council in 2016, maintaining it (including ecologist-advised dye treatments against weed growth) in exchange for use.

**Business reading:** marine RC has essentially **no commercial layer** — it is clubs on public water. Costs that RC WORLD must internalize are visible in the club record: weed and water-quality management, wildlife conflicts, wind exposure, retrieval of dead boats. Lessons for the Marine Division (Volume 6): purpose-built shallow ponds with engineered edges and retrieval design (the marine cousin of the Tow-Truck Protocol is Volume 6's retrieval tug — friction converted to gameplay again); scale-sailing and steering courses rent well because boats are slow, robust, and legible to spectators; and the century-long club record proves the activity's durability while its zero-commercialization proves the open field.

## 9. Customer Segmentation & Demand Analysis

### 9.1 The market RC WORLD actually competes in

The park's revenue does not come out of the ~$1.5–6.2 B RC product market; it comes out of **location-based entertainment (LBE) spend**. Industry analysts (2025–2026) size the global family-entertainment-center market at roughly **$30–40 B (2025)** growing **7–14% CAGR** — another legitimately wide range driven by scope differences — with attractions-industry reporting citing over a billion annual global visits to location-based entertainment. Two structural LBE trends favor the concept: the shift toward **participatory** formats (guests doing, not watching) and **multi-revenue-stream venues** (admission + F&B + retail + events per visit). RC WORLD is both by construction.

### 9.2 The five segments

**1. Families (primary volume segment).** Parents seeking a 2–4 hour weekend activity for children ~6–15; purchase driver is the parent, experience driver is the child; the Family Bundle (4 drivers × 2 Shifts, $89 canon) is built for them. Comparable spend per verified precedents: A$65 at Dig IT, ~$50/head at Diggerland with extras, $200+ birthday parties at US RC tracks. Demand character: weekend/holiday peaked; weather-sensitive until Phase 3's indoor arena; birthday parties (canon from $349) are the repeat-acquisition engine — every party delivers ~10 trial users.

**2. Hobbyists & enthusiasts (frequency segment).** Existing RC owners plus lapsed adults ("I had a Tamiya in 1995"). They come for groomed tracks, timing systems, events, and The Works; they buy memberships (Apprentice $29 / Operator $59 / Foreman $99 monthly, canon), own-equipment track passes, parts, and coaching. Reference behavior: $185/month unlimited memberships sell at plain Californian club tracks; drift tourists book hotel nights around Super-G. Modest headcount, outsized revenue-per-head and community value: they populate leagues, teach informally, and legitimize the venue.

**3. Corporate & groups (yield segment).** Team-building buyers currently choosing karting, escape rooms, axe-throwing. RC WORLD's pitch is unique mechanics: excavator-and-dumper *logistics challenges* (the 1:3 doctrine as a literal teamwork exercise), pit-crew races, telemetry-scored leaderboards on the big screens. Canon pricing from $1,400 (2 h, 20 pax) sits well under Dig This's corporate digs while offering larger simultaneous capacity. Weekday daytime demand — precisely when families are absent — makes this segment the utilization flywheel.

**4. Schools & STEM (weekday-volume and legitimacy segment).** Curriculum-linkable content is genuinely abundant: gear ratios and torque in the crawler park, energy management in LiPo doctrine, logistics arithmetic in the mining zone, embedded systems via the RCW Node. Priced as per-student programs with teacher materials (Volume 9, Chapter 7); the RC Academy (Phase 3) upgrades this to structured courses. Strategic value beyond revenue: municipal goodwill, grant eligibility, and the pipeline of future hobbyists.

**5. Tourists & event audiences (opportunistic segment).** Depends entirely on the site city's visitor economy; the Dig This model (a novelty attraction 20 minutes off the Strip) shows what a strong tourist market yields, but the base financial model (Volume 10) conservatively treats tourist revenue as upside, not base case — Grade D assumption flagged.

**Segment summary matrix:**

| Segment | Primary product | Canon price anchor | Demand window | Key conversion metric (KPI, Volume 10 Ch. 9) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Families | Casual Shifts, Family Bundle, parties | $15 Shift / $89 bundle / $349 party | Weekends, holidays | Party → repeat-visit conversion rate |
| Hobbyists | Memberships, own-equipment passes, events | $29–99/mo | Evenings, events | Member count & monthly churn |

A demographic note the whole plan should carry: every verified precedent that targeted *families* (Dig IT, Dig Dig Land, Diggerland, Fuji Subaru Land) reports or displays broad gender participation, while traditional RC club tracks skew heavily male-adult. The difference is framing — "operate machines in a world" versus "compete in a technical hobby." RC WORLD's positioning language, imagery, and staffing (Volume 9, Chapter 2) deliberately follow the family framing, because the addressable market is roughly double.

### 9.4 A worked TAM/SAM/SOM sketch for the reference site

The reference site is a mid-size international city (canon). Using deliberately conservative structure — every input Grade D and stress-tested in Volume 10:

- **TAM (catchment):** a metro of ~1.5–2.5 M within 60 minutes' drive. FEC-industry norms suggest 20–35% of households consume location-based family entertainment at least annually; at 2.7 persons/household this yields roughly **450,000–900,000 people in LBE-active households**, plus the corporate, schools, and tourist layers.
- **SAM (format-relevant):** the share for whom a vehicles/machines/skill proposition beats cinema-class alternatives — boys and girls ~6–15 and their parents, adult hobbyists and lapsed hobbyists, teambuilding buyers, STEM coordinators. Assumed at 25–40% of TAM: roughly **120,000–350,000 addressable individuals**.
- **SOM (Year 1):** the canonical Year-1 revenue of ≈$1.28 M at a blended ~$30–35 per visitor-visit (admission + F&B + retail mixed across segments) implies roughly **37,000–43,000 visits**. Against the SAM midpoint that requires penetrating on the order of **10–20% of addressable individuals once, or proportionally fewer with repeat visits** — demanding but consistent with what a well-marketed regional FEC achieves in its opening year, and far below the throughput ceiling of the Phase 1 plant (~150 assets × Shift arithmetic, Volume 3/4 capacity tables).

The sketch's purpose is honesty about magnitude, not precision: the plan does not require heroic penetration, and the binding constraint in Year 1 is expected to be *awareness and weekend capacity*, not addressable demand.

## 10. Market Positioning & SWOT

### 10.1 Where RC WORLD sits

Positioning statement: **the world's first full-scale miniature industrial world — a destination park where anyone can drive, dig, haul, race, sail, and fly real machines in miniature, without owning anything.** Against each adjacent category the position is differentiated, not merely better:

- vs. **hobby tracks**: a hospitality business with hobby-grade depth, not a hobby space tolerating guests;
- vs. **FECs/arcades**: real physics and real skill progression, not redemption games;
- vs. **karting**: comparable competitive thrill at lower price, zero physical risk, from age ~6, weather-extendable, and with construction/marine/aviation breadth karting cannot copy;
- vs. **Diggerland/Dig This**: 80–90% of the psychological payload of machine operation at 10–30% of the price and a fraction of the liability, insurance, and land cost;
- vs. **staying home with a $120 WLtoys buggy**: the world (tracks, mines, ponds), the fleet variety, the telemetry gamification, and the community — the same reason gyms coexist with home dumbbells.

### 10.2 Barriers to entry and defensibility

Honest assessment: **no single element of RC WORLD is patentable or secret.** Vehicles are catalogue items; tracks are earthworks. Defensibility is a *stack*:

1. **Operational know-how** — fleet balancing (1:3 doctrine), battery logistics at 450+ pack scale, 20-minute-Shift throughput engineering, repair-hour economics. This is exactly the know-how this Master Plan encodes, and it is genuinely hard-won (the German clubs took decades to learn their fraction of it).
2. **The telemetry/ERP layer** — RCW Node + RC WORLD OS delivers kill-switch safety, anti-abuse enforcement, queue automation, and per-asset economics no copycat gets from off-the-shelf parts quickly (Volume 13).
3. **Capital and land** — $1.85 M Phase 1 on 4.8 ha is modest for an attraction but far beyond hobby-shop copycats; the realistic fast-follower is an FEC chain, which would need years to acquire the RC-specific operating depth.
4. **Community and license lock-in** — Gears balances, License tiers, leaderboard history, and club partnerships create switching costs no new venue inherits.
5. **Brand-of-first-mover** in an emerging category (Section 8.1's window).

What defensibility does **not** include: exclusivity of supply (anyone can buy Huina), location monopoly beyond ~60–90 minute catchment, or IP moats. Franchise speed (Volume 12) is therefore itself a defensive strategy: occupy the category name before the trampoline-park consolidation moment arrives.

### 10.3 SWOT

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | **Strengths** — first-mover at destination scale in a validated emerging format; hobby-grade unit economics ($60–450 fleet assets vs $50k+ karting/real machines); multi-division breadth = repeat-visit variety + all-demographic reach; proprietary telemetry/ERP stack; safety story (nobody rides anything); founder domain expertise encoded in this plan | **Weaknesses** — no operating history (Grade D demand assumptions); weather exposure until Phase 3 indoor arena; single-region (China) supply concentration; novelty-dependence risk on repeat visits; staff skill bottleneck (Artisans must be trained, not hired ready-made); modest brand budget vs FEC incumbents |
| **External** | **Opportunities** — RC industry growth (5.8–7.5% CAGR) and hobby-grade shift feeding equipment quality down-market; LBE market growth (7–14% CAGR) and participatory-format trend; category consolidation play (franchise, Volume 12); corporate/STEM weekday demand largely unserved; media-friendliness of the format (every zone is a video set); club/community partnerships available cheaply | **Threats** — FEC or karting chain fast-follower after our proof-of-concept; tariff/trade disruption to Chinese supply (mitigation: Volume 8 dual-sourcing and spares depth); LiPo regulatory tightening (mitigation: bunker doctrine already exceeds norms); macro discretionary-spend downturn; land-lease insecurity (the classic club killer — mitigated by lease terms in Volume 1, Chapter 6); reputational risk from a single serious battery/injury incident |

### 10.4 Competitor-response scenarios

Positioning is only as good as its behavior under attack. Three scenarios, with prepared responses:

1. **FEC chain adds an "RC zone" (most likely, 2–4 years post-launch).** A trampoline-park or karting operator bolts a rental RC track onto an existing venue. Expected execution: toy-grade or low-hobby fleet, no telemetry, no progression, maintenance neglect leading to dead-vehicle rates that poison the experience. Response: publicize fleet uptime and lap-parity standards; accelerate License-program lock-in; treat their zone as category marketing that sends disappointed customers toward the real thing. The German club record (Section 5.2) and US track economics (Section 8.2) both show that under-maintained RC fleets fail visibly within months — this attack defeats itself unless the attacker buys the operating discipline, which is the expensive part.
2. **A funded copycat clones the full park in another city.** Not a threat to the reference site (LBE catchments rarely overlap at 60+ minutes) but a race for the category brand and franchise position. Response: the Volume 12 franchise timetable exists precisely to win this race; documentation depth (this plan) makes RC WORLD the franchisor of record before a copycat writes its first SOP.
3. **A major manufacturer goes direct-to-experience.** Tamiya already operates circuits; a Traxxas- or Horizon-branded park is conceivable. Assessment: manufacturers are structurally biased toward single-marque venues (Kakegawa's Tamiya-only rule) that under-serve the breadth a destination needs, and their 2025 retrenchment from community events (Section 6.1) signals appetite in the opposite direction. A manufacturer *partnership* — branded zones, fleet supply deals, sponsorship (Volume 1, Chapter 9's sponsorship strategy) — is the more probable and more valuable outcome; the park should court it.

### 10.5 Positioning risks we accept knowingly

Two final honesty items. First, the plan bets that **operation beats spectation** durably — that driving a miniature excavator stays compelling after the third visit in a way passive attractions do not; the precedents suggest yes, but only Year-1 cohort data will prove it. Second, the plan bets that **breadth is a moat rather than a focus-dilution** — that five divisions run to this plan's standards beat one division run twice as deep. The phasing (racing + construction first, canon Phase 1) is deliberately sequenced so that if the second bet weakens, the park is still a viable two-division business before Phase 2 capital commits.
