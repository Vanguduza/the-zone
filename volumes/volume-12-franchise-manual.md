## 1. Franchise Strategy & Rationale

### 1.1 Why franchise at all

RC WORLD's flagship park is designed to reach roughly $3.4 M revenue by Year 5 at a 24–28% EBITDA margin (Volume 10). That is a good business, but it is one park. The strategic question for Phase 3 and beyond is how the concept scales, and there are only three honest answers: build more owned parks, franchise, or do nothing. This volume argues for a **flagship-plus-franchise** model, for four reasons.

**Capital efficiency.** A full park consumes ≈ $3.9 M of capex over five years and ≈ $2.6 M of external funding (canonical figures, Volume 10). Owned expansion at three parks means raising and risking roughly $8 M of additional capital while management attention is split across construction projects in different cities. Franchising moves the site capex onto the franchisee's balance sheet and converts RC WORLD's growth into a royalty and technology-fee stream that carries software-like margins once the support organization exists.

**The product is already written down.** Most first-time franchisors fail at the same hurdle: the founder's business runs on tacit knowledge that was never documented, so the "system" being sold does not actually exist in transferable form. RC WORLD inverts this. The Master Development Plan was commissioned from day one as an investor prospectus, operations manual, and engineering handbook. Fleet balancing methodology (Volume 3), the excavator-to-dump-truck 1:3 ratio and haul-road doctrine (Volume 4), workshop rebuild procedures and trade hacks (Volume 7), the procurement network with named suppliers and landed-cost models (Volume 8), the RC WORLD License progression (Volume 9), the financial model (Volume 10), the park design standards (Volume 11), and the RC WORLD OS platform with role-based interfaces including a dedicated **franchisee role** (Volume 13) — these are the deliverables a franchisee is paying for. The license fee buys access to a documented, versioned, living system, not a handshake and a logo.

**Hard-to-copy assets travel well.** The defensible core of RC WORLD is not the land or the buildings — any competitor can lease a field. It is (a) the RCW Node telemetry hardware and kill-switch architecture, (b) RC WORLD OS and its operational data, (c) the wholesale procurement relationships built in Volume 8, (d) the engineering doctrine that keeps a rental fleet of ~150 powered assets at high availability, and (e) the customer-experience mechanics — Toolbox Talk, the Shift, the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, the License system. All five are *portable*: they cost the franchisor almost nothing to deliver to a second site, and they are exactly the things a would-be imitator cannot buy off a shelf.

**Local knowledge is genuinely local.** An RC park lives or dies on land acquisition, municipal relationships, local marketing, school and corporate networks, and weekend labor markets. A franchisee who owns those relationships in Kuala Lumpur or Dubai will outperform a head-office expatriate manager, and their own capital at risk aligns incentives in a way salaries never do.

### 1.2 What is genuinely replicable — and what is not

Franchise systems fail when they license things that do not transfer. The table below is the honest inventory, and it drives everything in Chapters 2 and 4.

| Asset | Replicable? | Transfer mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Park layout & civil design standards | Yes | Volume 11 design standards + franchisor design review gates (Chapter 7) |
| Fleet doctrine, models, ratios (1:3, 3:1 batteries, class ladder) | Yes | Volumes 3–6 + approved-equipment list, updated quarterly |
| Workshop practices, spares strategy, rebuild skills | Yes, with training | Volume 7 + Artisan certification at the flagship (Chapter 8) |
| Procurement channel (wholesale China network) | Yes | Central procurement desk (Chapter 11); Volume 8 as reference |
| RCW Node hardware & kill-switch logic | Yes, controlled | Nodes supplied only by franchisor; firmware signed; no design transfer |
| RC WORLD OS | Yes | SaaS license, multi-site tenancy (Volume 13, Chapter on franchise support) |
| Customer-experience canon (Toolbox Talk, Shift, Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, License, Gears) | Yes | Brand standards (Chapter 9) + OS enforcement |
| Brand & trademarks | Yes | License grant (Chapter 4) |
| Flagship's specific land economics & city demographics | **No** | Site criteria + local adaptation, never copied blindly |
| Founder's personal network & improvisation | **No** | Must be replaced by documentation and the Academy — this is the test of the whole plan |

### 1.3 The flagship-first proof requirement

RC WORLD will not sell a franchise on projections. The self-imposed, non-negotiable gate is:

> **No franchise will be offered until the flagship park has a minimum of two full years of operating data**, including at least one complete seasonal cycle at Full Park scope (Phase 2), audited financials demonstrating the unit economics claimed in disclosure materials, and a documented safety record.

Sequenced against the canonical phasing — Phase 1 (months 0–12) builds the Core Park, Phase 2 (months 13–30) completes the Full Park, Phase 3 (months 31–60) includes the franchise pilot — this means the earliest a franchise agreement is *signed* is around month 36, and the pilot park opens around months 52–58 after its own build (Chapter 12). This discipline costs perhaps a year against an aggressive timeline and buys three things: credible disclosure numbers (a legal necessity in most regimes — Chapter 4), a functioning RC WORLD Academy with real classrooms (a canonical Phase 3 build item), and a flagship that has already survived the failure modes a franchisee will meet.

### 1.4 Franchise versus owned expansion — the decision framework

Franchising is the default growth mode, not the only one. The board should apply this rule set per opportunity:

- **Own** when the market is the flagship's home region (support costs near zero, brand risk highest, learning value highest), when a strategic site becomes available at exceptional terms, or when a format experiment (e.g., the first indoor arena) needs full control to iterate.
- **Franchise (single-unit or area development)** when the market is proven analogous to the flagship's, a qualified operator exists, and the support organization can service the geography (Chapter 11).
- **Master franchise** only for distant, regulated, or culturally distinct territories where a capable local partner adds more than the ~50% royalty share they cost (Chapter 3.4), and never before the domestic pilot has operated for at least a year.

> **Investor Note.** The franchise program converts RC WORLD from a single-asset leisure operator into a system business. At maturity, each full-format franchise contributes roughly $230–250 k per year to the franchisor in royalties, technology fees, and procurement margin against a marginal support cost of $60–90 k (Chapter 5.3). The equity story investors are buying in the $2.6 M flagship raise includes this option value — but the option is only exercisable if the flagship performs, which is why the two-year proof gate is written into this volume rather than left to management discretion.

## 2. Franchise Models & Formats

### 2.1 Format philosophy

The flagship is a 4.8-hectare, four-division park in a mid-size international city. Very few franchise markets will replicate those conditions exactly: urban land is scarcer and dearer, northern climates cannot run outdoor divisions year-round, and some catchments cannot support a full park's revenue requirement. The franchise system therefore offers **three engineered formats**, each a defined subset or adaptation of the flagship specification — not improvisations. A franchisee licenses a format; deviations go through the local-adaptation process (Chapter 9.5).

All three formats share the invariant core: the Shift billing unit, Toolbox Talk induction, the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, the RC WORLD License and Gears programs, RCW Node telemetry on every powered asset, the bunkered charging room, the 3:1 battery ratio, electromechanical (lead-screw) rental construction fleet with hydraulics reserved for premium supervised experiences, and RC WORLD OS as the sole operating platform.

### 2.2 Format A — Full Park (4–5 ha)

The reference format: a licensed replica of the flagship at Full Park scope. Motorsport Division (Tracks A/B/C), Construction Division with Mining and Agriculture Zones, Aviation Division (netted airfield), Marine Division (pond complex), The Works, entry pavilion, restaurant, retail, events lawn. The franchisee builds in two compressed phases (Core Park then Full Park, typically 24–30 months to full scope rather than the flagship's 30) because the design is already proven — no experimentation budget, no layout iteration.

Fleet at maturity ≈ **195 powered assets** (the flagship's ~150 Phase 1 baseline plus ~20 aircraft and ~24 vessels). Mature revenue potential is benchmarked at 85–100% of the flagship's Year 5 canon ($3.4 M), i.e., **$2.4–2.9 M by the franchise's Year 3–4 and $2.9–3.4 M at full maturity**, depending on catchment. Suits peri-urban land in cities of 1.5 M+ metro population.

### 2.3 Format B — Compact Urban (~1.5 ha)

A dense, two-division park for expensive urban and inner-suburban land: **Motorsport Division plus Construction Division only** — Tracks A and B (Track C crawler trail in a compressed 400 m² technical course), Mining Zone, a reduced Agriculture Zone (optional; default is Mining plus crawler course), The Works at 60% flagship floor area, entry pavilion with F&B kiosk and retail counter, no restaurant, no aviation, no marine. This is deliberately the flagship's proven Phase 1 "Core Park" scope, densified onto 1.2–1.8 ha.

The compact format trades breadth for footfall: an urban catchment of 1 M+ within 30 minutes supports higher utilization per asset than the flagship's peri-urban site. Fleet ≈ **115–125 powered assets** (motorsport 64, construction and mining ~30, crawlers 16, recovery 4, plus utility). Single-phase build, 9–12 months. Mature revenue potential **$1.6–1.9 M** — above the flagship's $1.28 M Year 1 (which was a ramp year at the same scope) because the format assumes a denser catchment and opens with the benefit of every lesson the flagship learned.

### 2.4 Format C — Indoor Arena (cold climates)

For markets where an outdoor season shorter than ~7 months breaks the outdoor formats' economics (Scandinavia, Canada, northern US, parts of Central Europe) — and equally viable in extreme-heat markets as a climate-controlled variant (Chapter 3.3). A leased industrial building of **6,000–9,000 m²** with 6 m+ clear height houses: an indoor asphalt/drift circuit (Track A specification, Volume 3 Chapter 10 indoor annex), an indoor off-road course on engineered soil trays, a Mining Zone under dust management, a crawler course exploiting vertical structures, The Works, charging bunker, F&B kiosk, party rooms. No aviation (ceiling and netting constraints limit it to micro/indoor classes as an optional premium module), no marine beyond an optional 100 m² shallow pool module.

Fleet ≈ **90–110 powered assets**. The format's structural advantage is **weather-independent, 12-month utilization and evening trade** (indoor lighting is cheap; the flagship needed Phase 3 night-racing lighting to unlock evenings). Its structural burdens are rent, HVAC, and dust control. Mature revenue potential **$1.2–1.6 M**.

### 2.6 Format upgrade paths

Formats are starting points, not cages. Two upgrade paths are pre-engineered so that a successful franchisee grows inside the system rather than outside it:

- **B → A ("Compact-to-Full").** A compact urban franchisee whose unit sustains ≥85% of Format B's mature revenue band for four consecutive quarters may apply to develop a Full Park in an adjacent territory (their protected territory extends accordingly) at a reduced initial fee of $100,000, crediting their proven operating record against the standard discovery process. The compact unit continues operating — the formats serve different visit occasions and cannibalization at ≥20 km separation is expected to be minimal, to be verified with pilot data.
- **A + indoor module ("all-weather upgrade").** A Full Park franchisee in a seasonal market may add an indoor arena module (a scoped subset of Format C, typically 2,500–4,000 m²) on or adjacent to their site, mirroring the flagship's own Phase 3 indoor all-weather arena. This is treated as a design change through gates G1–G4, not a new franchise; no additional initial fee, fees continue on the enlarged revenue base.

Downgrades are not offered: a park that cannot sustain its format's economics is a support case (Chapter 10.4), not a re-scoping exercise.

> **Investor Note.** The compact urban format is the expected workhorse of the system: lowest absolute ticket, fastest build, and the densest revenue per hectare — and it licenses only the flagship's Phase 1 scope, which by franchise launch will have three-plus years of operating history. The full park is the flagship-equivalent statement piece for master franchisees; the indoor arena is the climate unlock. The pilot franchise (Chapter 12) should be a Format B unless the pilot candidate's market argues decisively otherwise.

## 3. International Expansion Strategy

### 3.1 Market prioritization framework

Markets are scored before any franchisee conversation begins, on four weighted axes plus a regulatory modifier. Scoring is maintained in RC WORLD OS as a living dataset; the table below defines the rubric.

| Axis | Weight | What is measured | Data sources (category) |
|---|---|---|---|
| RC culture depth | 30% | Hobby-grade RC penetration: club counts, track counts, hobby-shop density, RC e-commerce volume, event calendars | Hobby trade associations, marketplace data, club registries (2025–2026) |
| Disposable income | 25% | Household discretionary leisure spend; propensity to pay $15–59 per experience visit | National statistics, leisure-industry analysts |
| Land cost & availability | 20% | Achievable lease terms for 1.5–5 ha peri-urban or 6,000+ m² industrial | Local brokerage surveys |
| Climate | 15% | Outdoor operating days per year; heat/cold/monsoon profile; match to format A/B/C | Meteorological normals |
| Regulatory ease (modifier) | 10% | Franchise-law burden, foreign-ownership rules, licensing of amusement operations | Local counsel briefings — verify locally |

A market scoring below 60/100 is deferred regardless of franchisee enthusiasm. The global context supports patience: industry analysts (2025–2026) place the global RC car market between $1.5 B and $6.2 B depending on scope, growing 5.8–7.5% annually, with hobby-grade share rising — the addressable base is expanding in every priority region (Volume 2 carries the full market analysis).

### 3.2 Target region analysis

**Gulf states (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait).** Highest disposable income in the candidate set, strong motorsport culture (full-scale and RC), deep corporate-events demand, and government appetite for entertainment investment. Constraints: summer heat makes June–September outdoor operation marginal, so the indicated formats are Format C (climate-controlled arena) or Format A with an indoor arena built in phase one rather than as a later option, plus night operations as the default summer mode. Land is available but often via government-linked developers, favoring a **master franchise or area-development deal with a local entertainment group**. Foreign-ownership and commercial-agency rules vary sharply by emirate/state — verify locally. Priority: **first international wave**.

**Southeast Asia (Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, Philippines).** The deepest grassroots RC culture per dollar of income in the world — crawler and drift scenes are mass hobbies — plus low land costs and year-round warmth. Constraints: monsoon seasons demand the Volume 11 drainage standards without compromise plus covered pit lanes; price points must localize toward the bottom of the pricing governance band (Chapter 9.3); currency and royalty-repatriation mechanics need structuring. Indicated formats: A and B. Entry mode: single-unit or area development with hands-on franchisor support; master franchise only for archipelago markets (Indonesia, Philippines) where geography defeats direct support. Priority: **first international wave (Malaysia/Thailand), second wave (others)**.

**Europe (Germany, UK, Netherlands, Nordics, Poland).** Mature hobby markets with strong club infrastructure and high willingness to pay, but high labor costs, short outdoor seasons in the north, and demanding planning/permitting regimes. Indicated formats: C in the north, A/B in Iberia and southern France. EU pre-contractual disclosure rules and national franchise laws (e.g., France) apply — verify locally. Priority: **second wave**, led by an indoor-arena pilot in Germany or the Netherlands.

**North America (US, Canada).** The largest RC market and the most franchise-literate consumer base, with abundant peri-urban land in secondary metros. Constraints: the US is the most heavily regulated franchise-disclosure regime on earth (FDD under the FTC Franchise Rule plus state registration states — Chapter 4.2), liability-insurance costs are materially higher, and competition for family leisure spend is intense. Indicated formats: A in the Sun Belt, C in the north and Canada. Entry: **area development with experienced multi-unit operators**; do not enter the US before the franchise system has at least three operating units elsewhere and a US-counsel-prepared FDD. Priority: **third wave**.

### 3.3 Localization requirements

Every franchise adapts along four sanctioned dimensions; everything else is standard.

**Language.** RC WORLD OS ships with full internationalization: customer and staff apps, Toolbox Talk modules, License theory tests, signage packs, and waivers are translated and legally re-reviewed per market (Volume 13). Toolbox Talk voiceover and safety signage must be in the local language *and* English; the waiver must be in the contract language local counsel specifies — verify locally.

**Climate engineering — hot markets.** Shade sails over all spectator and driver rostrum areas; misting lines at queue points; charging-bunker ventilation upgraded (LiPo charging in 45 °C ambient requires forced airflow and tightened charge-rate limits per Volume 7 battery doctrine); track-surface temperature monitoring (tire compound shifts above ~55 °C surface); summer schedule pivots to 16:00–23:00 with night-racing lighting; battery storage air-conditioned to ≤28 °C (82 °F).

**Climate engineering — cold markets.** Format C as baseline; humidity control 40–55% RH for static and dust management; floor-level heating economics reviewed at design gate G2; entrance airlocks; winter tire compounds for any outdoor shoulder-season operation; LiPo cold-storage doctrine (packs warmed to ≥15 °C / 59 °F before charge or discharge).

**Cultural calendar.** The events and marketing calendar (Volume 9) re-anchors to local rhythms: Ramadan evening-hours mode in Gulf markets (peak trade after iftar); Lunar New Year and Songkran programming in Southeast Asia; European school-holiday matrices; local motorsport calendar tie-ins. Pricing promotions follow the local calendar within the governance bands of Chapter 9.3.

### 3.4 Entry modes

| Mode | Structure | When used | Fee mechanics (summary; detail Ch. 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-unit franchise | One franchisee, one site, direct franchisor support | Domestic market and first units in supportable near markets | Standard fees per Chapter 5.1 |
| Area development | One franchisee commits to 3–5 units on a schedule in a defined territory | Proven markets, capable multi-unit operators (esp. North America, Gulf) | Standard fees per unit; development fee = 50% of each committed unit's initial fee, paid upfront, credited at signing; units forfeit if schedule missed |
| Master franchise | Local partner buys territory rights, sub-franchises, delivers first-line support | Distant/regulated/culturally distinct territories; only after domestic pilot has ≥12 months of operation | Territory fee $250 k–$1.2 M by market size; unit initial fees and royalties split 50/50 franchisor/master; master must operate ≥1 unit itself first |

> **Field Note.** Several jurisdictions regulate *who may franchise at all*: China, for example, has historically required a franchisor to have operated two directly-owned units for at least one year before franchising ("2+1 rule"), and several Gulf states route foreign franchises through commercial-agency registration with strong agent protections. These rules can dictate entry mode regardless of commercial preference — another reason the master-franchise route exists in the toolkit. Verify each market with local counsel before any negotiation.

## 4. Licensing & Legal Framework

### 4.1 What the license grants

The franchise agreement grants, for the term and territory, a non-exclusive-brand / exclusive-territory license comprising:

1. **Brand.** Use of the RC WORLD name, logos, trade dress, division names (Motorsport Division, Construction Division, Aviation Division, Marine Division), and canonical program names (The Works, Shift, Toolbox Talk, Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, RC WORLD License, Gears) strictly per the brand standards manual (Chapter 9).
2. **RC WORLD OS access.** A multi-tenant SaaS subscription covering every platform module — bookings, payments, POS, fleet management, live telemetry, maintenance logs, HR/payroll, finance, CRM, reporting — with the franchisee role-based interface and mandatory franchisor read-access to operational and financial KPIs (Volume 13). Software is licensed, never sold; no source code transfers.
3. **Procurement network access.** The right (not obligation, except for controlled items) to buy through the central procurement desk at disclosed terms (Chapter 5.5); the approved-equipment list; supplier introductions per Volume 8. **Controlled items — RCW Nodes, node firmware, and License/badge credential stock — may be sourced only from the franchisor.**
4. **Manuals and training.** Licensed access to the Master Development Plan operating volumes (versioned, watermarked, confidential), all SOPs and checklists, and the RC WORLD Academy programs (Chapter 8).
5. **Ongoing system improvements.** Engineering bulletins, OS updates, new formats and products, marketing kits (Chapter 11).

Explicitly **not** granted: equity in RC WORLD; RCW Node schematics, PCBA design files, or firmware source; RC WORLD OS source code; the right to sub-franchise (master franchisees excepted); the right to operate any competing RC venue during the term plus a post-term period counsel confirms is enforceable locally.

### 4.2 Disclosure obligations — verify locally

Most priority markets impose pre-sale disclosure duties on franchisors, and breach typically gives franchisees rescission rights. Treat the following as a planning map, not advice:

- **United States:** a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) under the FTC Franchise Rule, delivered at least 14 days before signing or payment, with 23 prescribed items including audited franchisor financials and litigation history; a dozen-plus states add registration or filing requirements. Financial performance representations (Item 19) may only present substantiated data — which is precisely what the flagship-first proof requirement (Chapter 1.3) exists to generate.
- **Canada, Australia:** province-level disclosure laws (e.g., Ontario) and the Australian Franchising Code of Conduct respectively, each with their own disclosure documents, cooling-off periods, and good-faith duties.
- **EU and UK:** no single franchise statute, but national laws (e.g., French pre-contractual disclosure), competition-law limits on pricing and territory clauses, and data-protection law (GDPR) governing the customer data RC WORLD OS processes.
- **Gulf and Asia:** a patchwork of franchise-specific statutes (e.g., Saudi Arabia's franchise law), commercial-agency regimes, foreign-investment approvals, and registration duties.

Budget line: the franchisor legal build (first FDD-equivalent, trademark filings, template agreements) is carried in the Phase 3 franchisor budget (Chapter 11.1) at $120–180 k before the first international market, and each new country adds $25–60 k — engage franchise counsel per market and never reuse a disclosure document across borders.

### 4.3 Term, renewal, termination

Design intentions, to be conformed to local law:

- **Term:** 10 years, matching the depreciation life of the major civil works, with one 10-year renewal at the franchisee's option conditional on: audit scores ≥850/1000 average over the prior three years (Chapter 10.2), refurbishment to then-current standards, and re-signing the then-current agreement form. Renewal fee: 25% of the then-current initial fee.
- **Franchisor termination for cause:** unremedied safety-critical breach (immediate, after a single 7-day cure notice for anything not endangering people; zero cure period for knowing operation of a condemned asset class or disabling of RCW Node kill-switch functions); insolvency; abandonment (>14 consecutive unauthorized closed days); persistent under-reporting of gross revenue discovered by audit (Chapter 10); unauthorized transfer. Termination rights are among the most locally variable provisions in franchise law — several regimes require notice, cure, or even compensation regardless of contract wording; verify locally.
- **Franchisee exit:** sale of the business permitted with franchisor approval of the buyer (same selection standards as Chapter 6), a transfer fee of $25 k covering re-training and due diligence, and a franchisor right of first refusal on any sale.
- **Post-termination:** de-branding within 30 days, OS access revoked (customer-data handling per data-protection counsel), controlled items repurchased at book value at franchisor option, non-compete as locally enforceable.

### 4.4 Territory protection

Each agreement defines a **protected territory** — the greater of a 30 km radius or the 45-minute drive-time catchment around the site, adjusted at signing for geography — within which the franchisor will neither operate nor license another RC WORLD park of any format. Carve-outs: temporary event activations (mall pop-ups, expo circuits) under 30 days; e-commerce and app sales, which are system-wide by nature (retail revenue attribution for in-territory customers is credited per the OS attribution rules, Volume 13). Territory clauses interact with competition law in the EU and several other regimes — verify locally.

### 4.5 IP protection

- **Trademarks.** "RC WORLD," the logo, "RCW Node," "RC WORLD OS," "Toolbox Talk," "Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol," and "Gears" filed in home market before franchise launch, then internationally (Madrid Protocol where available) in every priority market *before* entering negotiations there — trademark squatting in first-to-file jurisdictions (notably China, where the supply chain already operates) is the single most common franchisor IP loss; file early, in local script where applicable.
- **RCW Node designs.** Layered protection: registered designs where cost-effective, but primarily **trade-secret handling plus supply-chain control** — franchisees receive assembled, conformally-coated, serialized nodes, never design files; fabrication remains with the franchisor's contract manufacturers (Volume 8); firmware is signed and encrypted, flashed only by the franchisor; nodes phone home to the franchisee's tenancy and cannot be re-provisioned outside RC WORLD OS.
- **Software.** RC WORLD OS is licensed SaaS; no on-premise deployment except where data-residency law forces it (then containerized, franchisor-managed, with counsel-approved terms). Master franchisees may negotiate source-code escrow releasable only on franchisor insolvency — grant reluctantly and narrowly.
- **Manuals.** Watermarked per licensee, confidentiality obligations surviving termination, and staged access: prospects see summaries; only signed franchisees see full volumes.

> **Investor Note.** The licensing posture is deliberately asymmetric: the franchisee gets everything they need to *operate* and nothing they would need to *defect*. Brand, OS tenancy, node supply, and procurement access all terminate with the agreement, which converts the franchise network into a durable annuity rather than a training program for future competitors.

## 5. Franchise Economics

### 5.1 Fee structure

The fee architecture follows one principle: **the franchisor earns when the franchisee earns.** Initial fees are set to cover onboarding costs plus a modest margin — not as a profit center — so that franchisor incentives stay aligned with long-run unit performance rather than unit sales.

| Fee | Amount | Basis & timing | What it pays for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial franchise fee | Format A $150,000 · Format B $90,000 · Format C $110,000 | One-time, at signing | License grant, site-approval process, design review, Academy training for franchisee + 2 leaders, pre-opening support team (Ch. 8.4) |
| Royalty | **6% of gross revenue** | Monthly, auto-calculated from RC WORLD OS revenue ledger | System license, ongoing support, engineering updates, manuals |

Total recurring load on the franchisee is therefore **8% of gross (royalty + marketing fund) plus a technology fee that lands at 1.4–2.4% of gross** depending on format and maturity — roughly 10% all-in, at the moderate end of experience-economy franchising. Gross revenue is defined in the agreement as all revenue recognized in RC WORLD OS excluding sales taxes and the marketing fund is a pass-through, not franchisor income. Because every booking, POS transaction, and payment runs through RC WORLD OS (Volume 13), **royalty under-reporting is structurally difficult** — the ledger the royalty is computed from is the same ledger the park runs on.

**Sensitivity.** The worked example moves most on three levers, in order: **utilization** (each ±10% on billed Shift-equivalents moves post-fee EBITDA by roughly ±$95 k, because Shift revenue carries minimal marginal cost), **labor ratio** (each ±2 points of revenue moves EBITDA ±$35 k; the OS rostering module against demand curves is the control), and **rent** (fixed at signing — which is why site economics are interrogated at gate G0, not repaired later). A downside case at 85% of plan revenue and +2 points labor still clears ≈ $190 k post-fee EBITDA at maturity — uncomfortable but solvent, provided the franchisee respected the 60% maximum-debt rule in Section 6.4. Fleet-parts cost is deliberately *not* a savings lever: the audit's fleet-condition domain (Chapter 10.2) exists precisely because deferring maintenance flatters one quarter and destroys the next year.

> **Investor Note.** Read the worked example from the franchisee's chair before believing it from the franchisor's. An operator committing ~$1.8 M wants three things this system genuinely offers: a proven design (no experimentation capex), structurally honest reporting (the OS ledger), and a franchisor whose income is 80%+ recurring and performance-linked rather than front-loaded in initial fees. Franchise systems with the opposite profile — fat initial fees, thin support — churn franchisees and die by lawsuit. The fee architecture above is a deliberate position on that spectrum.

### 5.4 Pricing interaction

Fee percentages are levied on gross revenue *as locally priced*. ### 5.5 Procurement margin option via central purchasing

Volume 8's wholesale network (Huina/Kabolite, MST, MJX, LDRC, Double E, Hobbywing, Gens Ace/CNHL and peers, plus PCBA fabrication) is opened to franchisees through a **central procurement desk** (Chapter 11.2). The commercial logic is mutual: consolidated orders across the network reach volume tiers no single park can, the desk adds pre-shipment QC and freight consolidation, and the 8–12% handling margin still leaves the franchisee's landed cost **below what they could achieve alone** — typically by 10–20% against retail-adjacent sourcing. Purchasing through the desk is mandatory only for controlled items (RCW Nodes, credential stock) and for any item on the approved-equipment list where the franchisee cannot evidence an equivalent-spec alternative; everything else is priced to win the order on merit. The desk's margin is disclosed in the franchise agreement — undisclosed supplier rebates are a classic franchisor trust-killer and are contractually forsworn.

## 6. Franchisee Recruitment & Selection

### 6.1 Ideal franchisee profile

The franchise is an **operator's business with an engineering heart**, not a passive investment. The ideal candidate is an owner-operator or a tightly-held partnership where one principal commits full-time, with: P&L management experience leading 10+ staff in a customer-facing operation (leisure, hospitality, fitness, automotive retail); comfort with technical operations (they need not solder, but they must respect the people who do); local roots in the target catchment; capital per Section 6.4 without over-leverage; and genuine enthusiasm for the product — a franchisee who has never held a transmitter will not survive the Academy, and should not.

Explicit disqualifiers: pure financial investors with hired "manager will run it" structures (permitted only in area-development deals with a certified operating partner); candidates whose capital plan depends on selling before break-even; anyone unwilling to complete the full Academy program personally.

### 6.2 Selection scorecard

Scored by two assessors independently at stage 4 of discovery; minimum passing score 70/100 with no axis below half marks.

| Axis | Weight | Evidence examined |
|---|---|---|
| Operating track record | 25 | References, prior P&Ls, staff-retention history |
| Capital adequacy & plan | 20 | Verified liquidity, funding letters, stress-tested plan |
| Local market strength | 15 | Site pipeline, municipal and school/corporate network |
| Technical & systems aptitude | 15 | Academy pre-assessment, response to OS demo, workshop day performance |
| Culture & brand fit | 15 | Behavior with flagship staff and customers during park immersion day |
| Alignment on term & standards | 10 | Reaction to audit regime, pricing governance, controlled-item rules |

### 6.3 Discovery process stages

1. **Inquiry & pre-qualification** — application, capital self-declaration, NDA (week 0–2).
2. **System overview** — format summaries, fee disclosure summary, market scoring review (week 2–4).
3. **Flagship immersion day** — full day at the park including a workshop session with Artisans and a shift as a marshal shadow (week 4–8).
4. **Formal assessment** — scorecard interviews, capital verification, background checks (week 8–12).
5. **Disclosure** — statutory disclosure document delivered; mandatory local cooling-off periods respected — verify locally (week 12+).
6. **Business-plan defense** — candidate presents site pipeline and 5-year plan to the franchise committee (week 14–18).
7. **Award & signing** — agreement executed, initial fee paid, Academy enrollment (week 18–24).

The franchisor may exit the process at any stage without cause; the candidate's costs are their own except the immersion day, which the franchisor hosts.

### 6.4 Capital requirements

| Requirement | Format A | Format B | Format C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total cash funding requirement (Ch. 2.5) | $2.7–3.1 M | $1.6–1.95 M | $2.0–2.45 M |
| Minimum unencumbered liquid capital (≥40%) | $1.1 M | $700 k | $850 k |
| Minimum net worth (≥1.5× funding) | $4.2 M | $2.7 M | $3.1 M |
| Maximum debt share of funding | 60% | 60% | 60% |

Proof standards: bank confirmations, audited statements or accountant letters, and funding term sheets before stage 6. The franchisor does not lend to franchisees and does not guarantee franchisee debt.

### 6.5 Financing support short of lending

While the franchisor takes no credit risk, it materially improves franchisee bankability: lenders receive (with the candidate's consent) the disclosure document, the format design kit, the flagship's audited performance history, and — once the pilot operates — pilot actuals; the 10-year lease-plus-renewal requirement (Section 7.1) gives asset-backed lenders a security horizon; and the OS ledger gives lenders live covenant reporting no independent leisure startup can offer. In markets with government small-business loan schemes or tourism-investment incentives, the franchise committee maintains a per-market briefing note — prepared with local advisors, verify locally — flagging programs a candidate's plan should exploit.

> **Field Note.** The single best predictor of franchisee failure across the leisure industry is not competence — it is under-capitalization meeting a slow ramp. A candidate who plans to open with the minimum liquidity and "grow into" working capital will make the one decision that kills an RC park: cutting Artisan hours and maintenance spend in month 8, just as the fleet's honeymoon ends. The 40% liquidity floor and the maintenance-protected audit exist to make that decision structurally difficult. Hold the line on capital adequacy even for otherwise outstanding candidates.

### 8.3 Assessment standards

Every program ends in assessment: written (OS-delivered, question banks versioned with the manuals), practical (e.g., ATC candidates perform a timed gearbox rebuild, a battery-bunker incident drill, and an RCW Node replacement with telemetry re-binding verified live), and behavioral (FOC candidates deliver a Toolbox Talk to real flagship customers under observation). Pass mark 80%; one resit; a failed second attempt requires a new candidate — the Academy protects the network, not the individual enrollment.

### 8.4 Pre-opening on-site support team

For each opening, the franchisor deploys a **4-person opening team** — an operations lead, a lead Artisan, an OS/telemetry engineer, and a front-of-house trainer — on site for **14 days before opening and 7 days after**, covering: fleet scrutineering and parity verification, bunker and charging commissioning, OS go-live and payment-rail testing, marshal rehearsals with full Tow-Truck Retrieval drills, soft-opening supervision, and first-week stabilization. A second, 2-person visit at week 6–8 tunes rostering and maintenance cadence against real demand data. Both visits are funded from the initial fee; extensions bill at disclosed day rates.

### 8.5 Ongoing training

Quarterly engineering and product bulletins (Chapter 11.2) each carry a micro-module in the OS learning system with completion tracked per certified role; annual recertification per the curriculum table; and an annual **Franchise Convention** at the flagship combining recertification intensives, the advisory council plenary (Chapter 11.4), and the system's championship finals — the event that turns a contract network into a community.

### 8.6 Regional certification hubs

Flying every Artisan to the flagship does not scale past a handful of units. Once a region hosts three good-standing parks, the franchisor designates one as a **regional certification hub**: its Works is audited to training standard, its lead Artisan completes a train-the-certifier program at the flagship (40 h), and ATC and FOC programs can then run regionally under flagship-issued question banks and practical-assessment rubrics, with the franchisor moderating a sample of assessments remotely via recorded practicals and OS telemetry evidence. FLC and GMC remain flagship-only for the plan horizon — the leadership courses are as much enculturation as instruction, and the flagship *is* the culture. Hub hosts are compensated per trainee at published rates; hub status is reviewed at each annual field audit and is itself an earned, revocable standard.

## 9. Brand Standards

Photography standard: machines working, hands on transmitters, dirt and detail — never sterile catalog shots.

### 9.2 Naming conventions

Canonical names are used exactly and are never translated, abbreviated, or localized without written approval: **RC WORLD** (all caps in headings), the park styled "RC WORLD [City]"; divisions as Motorsport Division, Construction Division (incl. Mining & Agriculture Zones), Aviation Division, Marine Division; **The Works**; **Artisans**; the **Shift** (Casual Shift, Operator Shift); **RCW Node**; **RC WORLD OS**; **RC WORLD License**; **Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol**; **Toolbox Talk**; **Gears**. Local-language explanatory taglines may accompany, never replace, canonical names (approved via 9.5). The internal codename "Omni-Zone" is legacy and never appears customer-facing.

### 9.3 Customer-experience non-negotiables

Four mechanics define the brand promise and are contractually protected; disabling or diluting any of them is a material breach:

1. **Toolbox Talk.** Every new customer completes the digital induction and waiver before first operation — no verbal shortcuts, no paper bypass. Completion is enforced by the OS booking flow.
2. **Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol.** Customers never walk onto live tracks. Breakdowns are recovered by the customer piloting a winch-equipped recovery crawler under marshal supervision, per the flagship SOP. This is simultaneously the park's signature delight moment and its central safety control; it is audited hardest (Chapter 10).
3. **License and badge integrity.** RC WORLD License tiers, badges, and Gears (Volume 9) are earned, portable across every park in the network via the customer's OS profile, and never sold, gifted, or locally re-specified. A Foreman-tier member from the flagship walks into any franchise and is recognized; that portability is a system asset no single park may debase.
4. **Pricing governance bands.** Local pricing is set by the franchisee within franchisor-approved bands: **70–130% of the canonical anchors** (Casual Shift $15/$22, Operator Shift $26/$38, Day Pass $59, family bundle $89, memberships $29/$59/$99), adjusted per market via a published PPP-based index reviewed annually. Within the band, price relativities must be preserved (premium classes above standard; Operator Shift below 2× Casual — the pit-stop swap is part of the value). Outside-band pricing requires a written variance with market evidence.

### 9.4 Service standards

The flagship's service canon (Volume 9) applies verbatim: greeting-to-transmitter time targets, pit-lane etiquette, marshal-to-track ratios, party and corporate-event run sheets, complaint-resolution empowerment limits, and cleanliness standards for The Works viewing window — the workshop is on show, and a chaotic bench is a brand breach, not a housekeeping issue.

### 9.5 Local-adaptation approval process

Any deviation from format kits, naming, CX mechanics, pricing bands, or approved equipment follows one process: the franchisee submits a **Variance Request** in RC WORLD OS (what, why, evidence, duration, rollback plan); the franchise support office responds within 10 business days with approve / approve-as-trial / decline; trials carry measurement plans and sunset dates; approved adaptations that outperform are considered for system-wide adoption with credit to the originating franchisee — the network is also an R&D engine, and the process is designed to harvest, not merely police, local intelligence. Safety-relevant variances additionally require engineering sign-off; CX non-negotiables (9.3) are not variance-eligible.

## 10. Quality Systems & Compliance

### 10.1 Audit program structure

Two instruments on fixed cadence: a **quarterly self-audit** conducted by the franchisee's GM in RC WORLD OS (checklist with mandatory photo/telemetry evidence attached per item; ~4 hours), and an **annual field audit** conducted unannounced-within-a-window by a franchisor auditor over two days, covering the full scored standard plus financial-reporting verification. Self-audit results that materially contradict field findings are themselves a scored integrity failure.

### 10.2 Scored standards checklist

The audit scores 1,000 points across four domains; ≥850 is *good standing* (and the renewal threshold, Chapter 4.3), 700–849 triggers an improvement plan, <700 enters the escalation ladder. Safety items marked ✱ are pass/fail regardless of aggregate score. Representative items (full instrument in the audit workbook):

**Safety — 300 points**

- [ ] ✱ RCW Node kill-switch live-tested on sample vehicles from each class; under-voltage and geofence intercepts verified
- [ ] ✱ Charging bunker to standard: fire-rated construction, smart balance chargers only, LiPo-rated extinguishing media, no unattended charging outside bunker
- [ ] ✱ LiPo doctrine in force: 3.4–4.2 V/cell window enforced by OS, 3:1 rotation evidenced, damaged-pack quarantine and disposal log current
- [ ] ✱ Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol observed live: zero customer track incursions; marshal intervention drill passed
- [ ] Toolbox Talk completion rate 100% of first-time operators (OS report)
- [ ] Haul roads within 15° maximum incline (spot-measured); track barriers and netting (where applicable) to Volume 11 spec
- [ ] Incident log complete, injuries reported per SLA, corrective actions closed

**Fleet condition — 250 points**

- [ ] Fleet availability ≥ 90% of rated assets per operating day (OS trailing 90-day report)
- [ ] Motorsport parity within 2–3% lap-time spread per class (weekly parity audit records)
- [ ] Excavator:dump-truck ratio 1:3 maintained in live Mining Zone operation
- [ ] Preventive-maintenance compliance ≥ 95% of OS-scheduled tasks on time; maintenance logs complete with parts traceability
- [ ] Rental construction fleet electromechanical only; hydraulic (Kabolite-class) units confined to premium supervised experiences
- [ ] Critical-spares inventory at or above Volume 7 minimum lines; battery fleet within cycle-count retirement policy

**Service & customer experience — 250 points**

**Financial reporting & administration — 200 points**

- [ ] Royalty and fee remittances current; reconciliation clean vs OS ledger

### 10.3 KPI reporting through RC WORLD OS

Because every franchise runs on the same platform, KPI reporting is not a submission — it is a fact of the system. The franchisor's **multi-site dashboards** (Volume 13) surface, per park and network-wide: revenue per available Shift-hour, fleet availability, Shift utilization by class and daypart, parity spread, maintenance compliance, battery-fleet health, Toolbox Talk completion, License progression rates, NPS, labor ratio, and royalty base. Franchisees see their own park benchmarked against anonymized network quartiles — the single most effective performance tool a franchise system owns, because no operator tolerates being fourth-quartile at fleet availability once they can see it.

### 10.4 Non-compliance escalation ladder

| 1 — Advisory | Single audit domain 70–84% or isolated finding | Written findings, franchisee action plan | Findings closed in 30 days |
| 2 — Improvement plan | Score 700–849, or repeated Level 1 | Joint improvement plan, monthly OS-tracked milestones, support visit at franchisee cost | ≥850 at re-audit within 90 days |

### 10.5 Mystery-shopper program

Two visits per park per year by contracted independent shoppers using the network scorecard: booking friction, Toolbox Talk quality, transmitter handover, marshal attentiveness, a staged breakdown to experience the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol as a customer, F&B and retail interaction, and departure/loyalty prompts. Reports flow into the audit score and the network benchmark. Shoppers are rotated so no park is scored twice consecutively by the same individual.

### 11.1 Franchisor organization build-out (Phase 3)

| Academy Manager (part-time from existing training lead) | Month 36 | Formalizes flagship training into the certifiable curriculum |
| Procurement desk (extension of flagship purchasing, +1 coordinator) | Month 40 | Volume 8 network opened to franchise orders |
| Franchise Field Manager (first) | Month 46–50 | Opening-team lead for the pilot; becomes pilot's field auditor |
| OS multi-tenancy hardening | Months 31–44 | Volume 13 roadmap item: franchisee role, multi-site dashboards, tenancy isolation, marketing-fund ledger |

Total incremental Phase 3 franchisor opex before pilot revenue: ≈ $280–360 k, carried within the Phase 3 envelope and offset by the pilot's initial fee and first royalties (Volume 10 Phase 3 model). Support scales with the network: one field manager per 6–8 units in-region is the staffing ratio planning assumption.

### 11.2 Support functions

**Procurement desk.** Consolidated ordering across the Volume 8 network; quarterly approved-equipment list updates (models enter and exit as suppliers change — the list, not the manual, is the live authority); pre-shipment QC; RCW Node supply with serialized provisioning; emergency spares express channel with 72-hour dispatch for grounded-fleet situations.

**Engineering updates.** Quarterly engineering bulletins from The Works: fleet hardening revisions, failure-data findings from network-wide maintenance logs (the OS aggregates every park's `maintenance_logs`, so the network learns at network speed), node firmware releases (pushed centrally), battery doctrine updates, and safety directives — the last being mandatory-compliance items with stated deadlines.

**Marketing kits.** Seasonal campaign kits (assets, media plans, offer templates pre-cleared against pricing bands), the content library, launch playbooks for new parks, and PR support for openings. The 2% marketing fund pays for system-level brand work and the customer app's market presence; local media buying remains the franchisee's 3% local obligation.

**New-product rollouts.** New experiences validated at the flagship first (e.g., night racing, drift leagues, seasonal build-workshops), then packaged as rollout kits: equipment list, pricing guidance, staff micro-module, OS configuration, and marketing assets. Franchisees adopt on their own schedule except where a rollout supersedes a safety-relevant standard.

### 11.3 Support-desk operations

A tiered support model through RC WORLD OS: Tier 1 (OS usage, bookings, payments — response same business day), Tier 2 (fleet engineering, telemetry, parity problems — 48 h, escalating to The Works), Tier 3 (safety incidents — immediate, 24/7 on-call rotation). All tickets and resolutions are searchable network-wide, so the knowledge base compounds.

### 11.4 Franchisee advisory council

From five operating units, a **Franchisee Advisory Council** convenes: elected franchisee representatives (one per region once regions exist), meeting quarterly (remote) and annually in person at the Franchise Convention. Mandate: review the marketing-fund accounts (audited annually and presented to the council), consult on standards changes with 30-day comment periods before non-safety standards take effect, prioritize the OS feature backlog's franchise lane, and channel variance-process innovations toward system adoption. The council is consultative, not governing — but a franchisor that routinely overrides its council is burning the trust the whole model runs on, and the board should treat council dissent as a leading indicator.

### 12.2 Gates to scale: 1 → 3 → master territories

Scaling is gated, not scheduled — each gate is an evidence test, and the dates are planning markers only.

**Gate F1 (pilot validation, ≈ pilot month 12 / program month 66):** pilot audit score ≥850; pilot revenue and post-fee EBITDA within 15% of its approved plan; zero uncured safety findings; franchisee would re-invest (structured interview); franchisor support cost per unit within budget. **Pass → release up to 3 regional units** (mix of Formats B and C, at least one in a second country from the first-wave list) over the following 18–24 months. Fail → fix the system before selling anyone else a franchise; the pilot franchisee's remedies are defined in the pilot agreement's side letter (build-out support extensions, fee holidays — negotiated once, at signing, with counsel).

**Gate F2 (regional validation, ≈ 12 months after the third unit opens):** three-plus units at good standing; multi-site dashboards and support desk proven at n>1; procurement desk demonstrating landed-cost advantage; advisory council seated. **Pass → open master-franchise negotiations** for first-wave distant territories (Gulf, Southeast Asia archipelagos per Chapter 3), with Format A flagship-equivalents anchoring each master territory. This stage extends beyond the five-year plan horizon; it is sketched here so the earlier gates are built to serve it.

**Illustrative Year 5+ franchisor economics** (pilot mature + 3 regional units ramping, per Chapter 5.3 rates): recurring franchisor revenue ≈ $450–600 k/yr against support costs ≈ $280–350 k — roughly breakeven-to-modestly-positive, with the initial fees having funded the build. The franchise program's Year 5 contribution to the flagship company is therefore **strategic, not financial**: the financial payoff arrives in years 6–10 as units mature and count grows, which is exactly the honest message for investors and the reason this volume resists front-loading fees.

> **Investor Note.** The gate design encodes the program's core discipline: RC WORLD sells its second franchise on the strength of its first, not on the strength of its deck. If Gate F1 fails, the loss is one pilot relationship and roughly $300 k of franchisor build cost — survivable. If a 10-unit system fails, the brand fails everywhere at once. Slow is the cheap option.

## 13. Volume Summary & Cross-References

This volume converts the RC WORLD system into a replicable, licensable product on a disciplined timeline: **no franchise sold before the flagship has two full years of audited operating data** (earliest signing ≈ month 36, pilot opening ≈ month 54, inside the canonical Phase 3 window of months 31–60). Three engineered formats are offered — **Full Park (4–5 ha, ≈$2.7–3.1 M funding, ~195 assets)**, **Compact Urban (~1.5 ha, motorsport + construction only, ≈$1.6–1.95 M, ~120 assets)**, and **Indoor Arena (6,000–9,000 m², ≈$2.0–2.45 M, ~100 assets)** — all carrying the invariant core: the Shift, Toolbox Talk, the Tow-Truck Retrieval Protocol, License/Gears portability, RCW Node telemetry, LiPo doctrine, and RC WORLD OS as sole platform. The fee model is deliberately recurring-weighted: **initial fees of $90–150 k by format, 6% royalty, 2% marketing fund, and a technology fee of $2,000/month plus $8 per active asset**, with an 8–12% disclosed procurement margin via central purchasing. A worked Format B example shows a franchisee earning ≈ 18.7% post-fee EBITDA at maturity on an ≈$1.8 M investment, while each mature unit contributes ≈ $130–250 k of recurring revenue to the franchisor. Expansion proceeds through evidence gates — one pilot, then three regional units, then master-franchise territories — prioritizing the Gulf and Southeast Asia in the first international wave. All legal mechanics are design intentions to be implemented with local franchise counsel in every market.
